## Bài đọc 1 (Unit 5) — Ghi chép câu lạc bộ sách: The Great Gatsby

> **Book Club Notes: Can We Trust Nick Carraway?**
>
> Harriet's Book Club, first Tuesday of July. Book: The Great Gatsby (1925), by Fitzgerald. Notes by Lydia Park.
>
> We chose this novel partly because it is short and partly because, since 2021, it has been free for anyone to read, having entered the public domain in the United States. Few of us had read it since high school, and several members admitted that they remembered the parties far better than the plot.
>
> A reminder of the story, for anyone who skipped the reading. The hero, Jay Gatsby, a mysterious and extremely rich man, throws lavish parties at his house on Long Island in the hope that Daisy, the woman he loved five years earlier, will one day walk through the door. Daisy is now married to a haughty, contemptuous man named Tom. Every night Gatsby stares across the bay at a green light at the end of her dock. Fitzgerald's prose is ornate in places, and his portraits of the guests, who drink Gatsby's champagne while spreading lurid stories about where his money came from, are close to farcical. The story is told by Nick Carraway, Daisy's cousin, who rents the small house next door.
>
> Our liveliest argument was about Nick. On the very first page, he tells us that he is "inclined to reserve all judgments". Later he describes himself as one of the few honest people he has ever known. Ethan pointed out, to general laughter, that anyone who announces his own honesty is usually about to lie. Nathan went further: Nick judges everyone constantly, and the person he judges least is Gatsby, whom he plainly admires. In other words, the narrator who promises not to judge is, in fact, the novel's harshest judge, and its least reliable one.
>
> Harriet disagreed. To her, the book is less a story about one man than a parable about believing that you can rebuild your life exactly as it was. When Nick warns Gatsby that the past cannot be repeated, Gatsby is astonished: "Why of course you can!" "I have spent this year arguing that you cannot get back the old version of anything," she said, "so I am bound to find him tragic rather than foolish." Ethan, who had spent much of the evening quietly defending Gatsby, added that a man who stands alone at the end of his dock every night, staring at a light, is less a villain than a portrait of loneliness.
>
> We ended, as most readers do, with the famous last line: "So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past." Several of us found it lyrical and moving; Nathan found it beautiful but suspicious, "the kind of sentence you write when you want the reader to stop asking questions".
>
> A final irony that delighted the group: the book sold disappointingly when it first appeared, and Fitzgerald died in 1940 convinced, by most accounts, that it had failed. Only afterwards did it become one of the most widely read novels in America. Gatsby could not repeat the past; his author never saw the future.
>

### Câu hỏi

**1. Why did the book club choose The Great Gatsby?**
*(Vì sao câu lạc bộ sách chọn The Great Gatsby?)*

- A. because it was a new novel that had just come out and all the members were eager to read it *(vì đó là một tiểu thuyết mới ra mắt và mọi thành viên đều háo hức đọc)*
- B. because Harriet had never read it and wanted to discuss it with others *(vì Harriet chưa từng đọc và muốn thảo luận với mọi người)*
- C. because it is short and, being in the public domain, free for anyone to read *(vì nó ngắn và, do thuộc phạm vi công cộng, ai cũng đọc miễn phí được)*
- D. because it is about San Diego and the people who live near Maple Street *(vì nó viết về San Diego và những người sống gần phố Maple)*

**2. What does the remark that members "remembered the parties far better than the plot" suggest?**
*(Lời nhận xét các thành viên "nhớ các bữa tiệc rõ hơn nhiều so với cốt truyện" gợi ý điều gì?)*

- A. The novel has no plot, so the members remembered only the parties. *(Cuốn tiểu thuyết không có cốt truyện, nên các thành viên chỉ nhớ các bữa tiệc.)*
- B. The members went to many parties when they were at school together. *(Các thành viên đã dự nhiều bữa tiệc khi cùng học ở trường.)*
- C. The parties are the most important part of the story, so they remember them. *(Các bữa tiệc là phần quan trọng nhất của câu chuyện, nên họ nhớ chúng.)*
- D. The book's vivid surface is more memorable than its deeper story. *(Bề mặt sống động của cuốn sách dễ nhớ hơn câu chuyện sâu bên dưới.)*

**3. Why does Gatsby throw his parties?**
*(Vì sao Gatsby mở tiệc?)*

- A. in the hope that Daisy will one day walk through the door *(với hy vọng một ngày Daisy sẽ bước qua cửa)*
- B. to make money from the guests who drink his champagne *(để kiếm tiền từ những vị khách uống sâm panh của anh)*
- C. to impress Nick, who has just rented the house next door *(để gây ấn tượng với Nick, người vừa thuê căn nhà bên cạnh)*
- D. because Tom asked him to entertain the guests from the bay *(vì Tom nhờ anh tiếp đãi những vị khách từ vịnh)*

**4. Why does Ethan's comment cause "general laughter"?**
*(Vì sao lời Ethan khiến cả nhóm bật cười?)*

- A. because Ethan told a joke about Daisy and her husband *(vì Ethan kể một câu đùa về Daisy và chồng cô)*
- B. because Ethan had not read the book and mixed up the narrator with Gatsby *(vì Ethan chưa đọc sách và nhầm người kể chuyện với Gatsby)*
- C. because his observation about self-declared honesty is funny but true *(vì nhận xét của anh về việc tự nhận mình trung thực vừa buồn cười vừa đúng)*
- D. because Nathan disagreed with him in a loud and funny voice *(vì Nathan phản đối anh bằng giọng to và hài hước)*

**5. According to Nathan, what is ironic about Nick as a narrator?**
*(Theo Nathan, điều trớ trêu ở Nick với vai trò người kể chuyện là gì?)*

- A. He knows nothing about Gatsby and has never spoken to him *(Anh chẳng biết gì về Gatsby và chưa từng nói chuyện với ông)*
- B. He promises not to judge, yet he judges everyone constantly and favors Gatsby. *(Anh hứa không phán xét, vậy mà phán xét mọi người liên tục và thiên vị Gatsby.)*
- C. He is Daisy's husband, yet he tells the story as if he were a stranger to her. *(Anh là chồng Daisy, vậy mà kể chuyện như thể là người lạ với cô.)*
- D. He never speaks, so the reader must guess what he thinks *(Anh không bao giờ lên tiếng, nên người đọc phải đoán anh nghĩ gì)*

**6. How does Harriet read the novel?**
*(Bà Harriet đọc cuốn tiểu thuyết theo cách nào?)*

- A. as a parable about believing you can rebuild the past exactly as it was *(như một ngụ ngôn về niềm tin rằng ta có thể dựng lại quá khứ y như cũ)*
- B. as a story about parties and champagne, told by someone who enjoys them *(như một câu chuyện về tiệc tùng và sâm panh, do người thích chúng kể lại)*
- C. as a comedy that makes fun of rich people on Long Island *(như một vở hài chế giễu người giàu ở Long Island)*
- D. as a true story about a man whom Fitzgerald once knew *(như một câu chuyện có thật về người mà Fitzgerald từng quen)*

**7. Why does Harriet say she is "bound to find him tragic rather than foolish"?**
*(Vì sao bà Harriet nói bà "hẳn phải thấy ông ta bi kịch chứ không phải ngốc nghếch"?)*

## Bài đọc 2 (Unit 6) — Bài Leah: khi khoa học tự kiểm tra lại mình

> **When Science Checks Its Own Work**
>
> Dr. Leah Carter, for the Sunset Bay Hospital staff newsletter
>
> A few years ago, I wrote in this newsletter about a famous idea in psychology, the bystander effect, and how a newer study had complicated it. Several readers asked me afterwards how often this happens. The honest answer is: more often than most of us were taught.
>
> In 2015, a group of around 270 researchers, working together as the Open Science Collaboration, published the results of an extraordinary project in the journal Science. They had tried to repeat one hundred psychology studies that had been published in three respected journals, following the original methodology as closely as possible. Of the original studies, 97 percent had reported a statistically significant result. Of the repeated versions, only 36 percent did, and the effects they found were, on average, about half as large. It is important to be precise about what this does and does not mean. A failed replication does not prove that the original finding was false; the second study might simply have been unlucky, or the people tested might have differed in some relevant way. What the aggregate result does show is that a published finding, on its own, was far less certain than many readers had assumed.
>
> One of the best-known casualties was "power posing". A 2010 study had reported that standing in a confident, expansive position for two minutes changed people's hormone levels and made them more willing to take risks. The idea spread rapidly, helped by an enormously popular online talk. Later and larger studies failed to find the hormonal effect, and in 2016 one of the original authors stated publicly that she no longer believed the effects were real.
>
> It would be easy to conclude from all this that psychology, or science in general, cannot be trusted. I think that conclusion is wholly mistaken. A field that spends years testing its own most celebrated findings, and then publishes the disappointing results in its leading journal, is not failing. It is doing exactly what science is supposed to do. The scientist who announced that she had changed her mind deserves more admiration, not less, than the many who never did.
>
> What the project does teach us is how to read a single study. Small samples produce dramatic results more easily than large ones. A surprising finding that has never been repeated is a hypothesis, not a fact. And a correlation reported in the news, such as a link between a food and a disease, may well be explained by a third factor that nobody measured; people who eat more vegetables, for instance, also tend to exercise more, sleep more and smoke less. In epidemiology, where researchers often cannot run experiments and must instead observe large groups over long periods, such confounding factors are the first thing a careful practitioner looks for.
>
> Medicine is not immune to any of this, and neither are we. The habit I try to keep is simple, if not always easy: when a study tells me exactly what I hoped to hear, that is the moment to read it most carefully.

## Bài đọc 3 (Unit 5) — Tiểu luận: thời tiết trong tiểu thuyết không bao giờ chỉ là thời tiết

> **Weather Is Never Just Weather**
>
> From a lecture on the craft of fiction
>
> In 1856, the critic John Ruskin gave a name to a habit he found in a great deal of poetry: the "pathetic fallacy", by which he meant giving human feelings to things that cannot possibly have them. His favorite example was a line of verse describing "the cruel, crawling foam" of the sea. The foam, Ruskin pointed out, not very patiently, is neither cruel nor crawling; it is simply water, and the cruelty belongs to the grief of the person watching it. He did not think the habit was always wrong, but he ranked the poets who relied on it below those who could feel deeply and still see clearly.
>
> Novelists have rarely taken his advice. Open almost any novel at a moment of crisis and the weather behaves with suspicious timing: a gale rises as the lovers quarrel, torrential rain falls on the funeral, and the sun breaks through just as the heroine forgives her sister. Read in bulk, the effect can be close to farcical, as if the sky had been hired to supply the emotions that the prose could not. Yet the best writers use weather less to decorate a feeling than to make an argument.
>
> Consider the opening of Charles Dickens's Bleak House, which appeared in monthly parts in the early 1850s. It begins not with a character but with two words, "Fog everywhere", and goes on for paragraphs: fog on the river, fog in the eyes and throats of old men by their fires, fog drifting into the kitchens of ships. Only then does Dickens reach the court at the dark center of the book, where a case about a will has dragged on so long that nobody alive can say what it was originally about. The fog is not there to make us feel gloomy; it is an allegory of the court itself, which hides the truth, chokes everyone who enters, and spreads into lives that never asked to be part of it.
>
> The novelist Thomas Hardy went further still. The first chapter of The Return of the Native (1878) contains no people at all, only a long, brooding description of Egdon Heath, a wide stretch of wild land under a sky growing dark. Readers who expect a story to begin with a person may squirm through these pages, but the delay is the point. By the time the characters appear, huddled against the wind or perched on its ancient hills, we have been made to feel how small and temporary they are, and how little the land will notice when they are gone. Hardy's heath does not share the feelings of the people who live on it; its indifference is precisely what makes their passions look both heroic and slightly absurd.
>
> This, I think, is where Ruskin's complaint needs to be refined rather than dismissed. The weak writer asks the weather to agree with the characters. The strong writer lets it disagree, or simply not care, and allows the reader to feel the gap. A wedding on a perfect afternoon tells us nothing; the same wedding, described by a narrator who knows that the marriage will fail, is a veritable lesson in irony.
>
> When you next write a storm into a story, ask yourself an unfashionable question: whose side is it on? If the honest answer is "the hero's", you may want to send it back out to sea.

## Bài đọc 4 (Unit 5) — Điểm sách châm biếm: mỗi câu văn là một chùm đèn pha lê

> **Every Sentence a Chandelier**
>
> A review of A Lantern for Lady Rowena, by Clarence Whitby (874 pages)
>
> There are novels that tell a story, and there are novels that hold a story prisoner; Clarence Whitby's fourth book belongs, magnificently, to the second kind. Its plot, which could be summarized on the back of a bus ticket, concerns a young woman who inherits a derelict mansion, a rickety bridge, and a family secret that the reader will sniff out somewhere around page forty. Lady Rowena takes a further seven hundred pages to catch up.
>
> Nobody, however, reads Whitby for the plot. They read him for the prose, which his fans describe as baroque and which I would describe as baroque with several additional rooms. No door in this novel is merely opened when it can instead be "surrendered, with a long and mottled sigh, to the grief of its hinges". No rain is allowed to fall without first becoming torrential, then lyrical, and finally, in a passage I read three times because I could not believe it, "a veritable cloister of weeping". There is a virtuoso quality to all this, in the way that there is a virtuoso quality to a man juggling chainsaws: one admires the skill while quietly wondering why he chose this particular activity.
>
> The characters fare less well. Rowena is the familiar archetype of the innocent heiress, and she does little except quiver, brood at windows, and wallow, at impressive length, in her own feelings. Her cousin Edmund, a haughty figure who lords it over the servants and delivers every line with a contemptuous curl of the lip, strings her along for most of the book before revealing himself, to nobody's surprise, as the villain. The only character with any life is the housekeeper, Mrs. Thorne, an unobtrusive presence whose dry remarks are so incongruous among the surrounding chandeliers that one suspects Whitby wrote them by accident.
>
> It would be unfair to deny that some passages are genuinely beautiful. A description of evening on the seaside promenade, with a plume of smoke rising from a distant ship and the last light shimmering on the wet stones, is as fine as anything Whitby has written. It is four sentences long, which may explain it.
>
> The publisher calls the book "an unsurpassed achievement in the modern novel", and in one sense this is wholly accurate: I cannot imagine anyone surpassing it, or wishing to try. I do not doubt that it will be a resounding success with readers who have the patience for a myriad of adjectives and nigh on nine hundred pages of them. For the rest of us, it offers an unadulterated pleasure of a different kind: the pleasure of closing it.
>
> Some readers will find this review venomous; I would prefer to call it disappointed. Mr. Whitby is plainly a gifted writer, and that is exactly what makes the book so frustrating. Somewhere under all that velvet there is a lean, unsettling novel of about two hundred pages, trying to get out. I hope that one day he lets it.

## Bài đọc 5 (Unit 6) — Phóng sự khoa học: hạt neutrino "nhanh hơn ánh sáng"

> **Sixty Billionths of a Second**
>
> How scientists handled a result they did not believe
>
> In September 2011, an international team working on an experiment called OPERA made an announcement that seemed to threaten the foundations of modern physics. Their detector, buried under a mountain in central Italy, had been recording tiny particles called neutrinos, sent through solid rock from CERN, near Geneva, about 730 kilometers away. According to the team's measurements, the neutrinos were arriving about sixty nanoseconds, or sixty billionths of a second, earlier than light itself would have done. If true, the result would have meant that something could travel faster than light, a possibility that Einstein's theory of relativity rules out.
>
> What made the announcement unusual was not the claim but the tone. The researchers did not say that they had overturned Einstein. Instead, they said, in effect, that they had spent months searching for a mistake in their own methodology, had failed to find one, and were now asking others to help. The velocity they had measured exceeded that of light by only about two and a half parts in a hundred thousand, a difference that a single faulty parameter could easily produce: the length of a cable, the timing of a clock, the position of the detector. Several of the team's own members had refused to sign the paper, believing that the result was not yet ready to be shown to anyone.
>
> Many scientists outside the team were openly skeptical. Their reasoning was not simply loyalty to a famous theory. Neutrinos had been measured before, most famously in 1987, when a star exploded in a nearby galaxy and its neutrinos reached detectors on Earth a few hours before its light. That small head start was expected, since light takes longer to escape from a dying star; but if neutrinos really moved as fast as OPERA suggested, those from the explosion should have arrived not hours but years ahead of the light. A hypothesis that explains one measurement while contradicting another is, in most cases, a sign that one of the measurements is wrong.
>
> The answer, when it came, was almost embarrassingly ordinary. In February 2012, the team reported two faults in its equipment, the more important being a fiber-optic cable that had not been properly connected to a computer card. The faulty connection delayed a timing signal and, as later tests showed, was large enough to explain the early arrivals. A second fault, in a clock oscillator, pushed the measurement slightly in the opposite direction. When the measurement was repeated that spring, and a separate experiment checked it independently, the results converged on the old answer: the particles had traveled no faster than light.
>
> Some newspapers told this as a story of failure; scientists tend to tell it differently. The team had a result it could not explain, adhered to the rules by publishing its data and method in full, and invited others to find the flaw, which the team itself then found within months and announced as openly as the original claim. The project's spokesman nonetheless resigned soon afterwards, which some colleagues regarded as a fair consequence and others as a harsh punishment for honesty.
>
> The astronomer Carl Sagan liked to say that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. The OPERA episode suggests a quieter lesson: that extraordinary claims also require extraordinary humility, and that a loose cable is a far more likely explanation for a miracle than a mistake by Einstein.

## Bài đọc 6 (Unit 6) — Bản ghi podcast sức khoẻ: hiệu ứng giả dược có thật không?

> **Second Opinion: Is the Placebo Effect Real?**
>
> A lightly edited transcript of a health podcast. The host, Colin Dunn, talks to Dr. Simon Avery, a medical statistician.
>
> **Colin:** Simon, most of us grew up believing that a sugar pill can cure people, provided they believe in it. Is that true?
>
> **Simon:** It is partly true, which is the most annoying kind of answer, I know. The famous figure comes from a paper published in 1955, which claimed that roughly a third of patients improved when they were given a placebo. That number has been quoted ever since, often as proof that the mind can heal the body.
>
> **Colin:** And it can't?
>
> **Simon:** The problem is what the number actually measures. If you give people a sugar pill and a third of them get better, you have not shown that the pill did anything. Many of those people would have recovered anyway, because most illnesses get better by themselves. And there is a less obvious effect called regression to the mean.
>
> **Colin:** Which is?
>
> **Simon:** Symptoms fluctuate. People tend to join a medical trial when they feel at their worst, because that is when they go to the doctor. Statistically, their next measurement is likely to be closer to their average, which means better, whatever you give them. It looks like a cure; it is mostly arithmetic.
>
> **Colin:** So how would you separate the real effect from the arithmetic?
>
> **Simon:** You need a third group, one that receives no treatment at all. In 2001, two researchers in Copenhagen did exactly that, analyzing more than a hundred trials that had included both a placebo group and an untreated group. When they compared the two, the placebo groups were, in aggregate, barely better off for most conditions. The clearest exception was pain, along with other symptoms that patients report themselves, where the placebo did seem to help a little.
>
> **Colin:** That sounds like bad news for the mind.
>
> **Simon:** Not quite. Pain is not a trivial exception; it is one of the main reasons people see a doctor. There is good evidence that expecting relief can reduce pain through real neural and physiological changes, involving the brain's own pain-relieving chemicals. And then there is the most puzzling study of all.
>
> **Colin:** Go on.
>
> **Simon:** In 2010, a team at Harvard gave about eighty patients with a painful bowel condition a bottle of pills and told them, quite honestly, that the pills contained no medicine at all. After three weeks, the patients taking them reported significantly more improvement than those who received nothing. Nobody fully understands why. One hypothesis is that the ritual itself, the bottle, the routine, the attention of a caring practitioner, does something that a pill alone cannot.
>
> **Colin:** So should doctors prescribe placebos?
>
> **Simon:** I would be cautious. Those results come from small samples and need replication. There is also a risk that people will hear "placebos work" and abandon treatments that genuinely do. What I would say is this: the placebo effect is smaller than its reputation, but kindness, time and a clear explanation are not nothing. If a sugar pill is an antidote to anything, it may be to a rushed appointment.

## 💡 Mẹo làm bài

- Biên bản nhiều người nói: ghi chú **ai nghĩ gì** (Ethan, Nathan, Harriet). Câu hỏi so sánh ý kiến hay bẫy bằng cách gán ý người này cho người kia.
- Sự trớ trêu (irony) C2 thường có dạng: điều nhân vật tuyên bố ↔ điều nhân vật làm. Tìm hai câu đối nhau trong bài.
- Bài khoa học: phân biệt `hypothesis` (giả thuyết, cần lặp lại) với `fact`, và `correlation` (tương quan) với nguyên nhân. Ví dụ "third factor" là chỗ hay hỏi chức năng.
- Người viết C2 hay **chặn kết luận cực đoan** (`It would be easy to conclude… wholly mistaken`). Phương án "science cannot be trusted" là bẫy.
- Điểm sách châm biếm: lời khen bề mặt (`magnificently`, `unsurpassed`, `resounding success`) thường là lời chê. Đọc câu kế tiếp để thấy cú chích (`It is four sentences long, which may explain it`).
- Tiểu luận phê bình: tìm câu tác giả **chỉnh lại** ý của người khác (`refined rather than dismissed`). Đáp án về quan điểm cuối thường nằm ở đó, không phải ở ý của Ruskin.
- Phóng sự khoa học: phân biệt **ví dụ giả định** (`could easily have been produced by…`) với **sự thật đã xảy ra** (`In February 2012, the team reported…`).
- Bản ghi hội thoại: theo dõi **câu hỏi của người dẫn** — mỗi lượt của chuyên gia trả lời đúng câu hỏi ngay trước (`So how would you separate…?` → `a third group`).
