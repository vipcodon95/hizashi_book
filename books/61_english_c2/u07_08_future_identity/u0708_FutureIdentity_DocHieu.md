## Bài đọc 1 (Unit 7) — Báo cáo sáu tháng thử tuần làm bốn ngày ở Bennett Studio

> **Six Months of Four-Day Weeks: What We Learned**
>
> Bennett Studio, internal report, September. Written by Chloe Bennett for the team and shared, with their permission, with two other small studios that asked about it.
>
> Why we tried it. The idea came from Ruby, who sent me an article about a trial run in the United Kingdom in 2022. Over six months, 61 companies with around 2,900 employees reduced working hours without reducing pay. At the end, 56 of them chose to continue, and 18 made the change permanent. I read the results with interest and some suspicion, since the companies had volunteered to take part and most of the figures were reported by the companies themselves. Nevertheless, they were striking enough to justify an experiment of our own.
>
> What we did. From March to August, the five of us worked Monday to Thursday, with full pay, on the principle that the work, not the hours, is what clients pay for. We cut our internal meetings in half and began using AI tools to automate the most tedious tasks, such as resizing a single design for twenty different screens. We also stopped farming out small jobs to freelancers at the last minute, which had always been the most expensive way to save time. One person was available by phone each Friday, on a rotating basis, in case a client had an emergency.
>
> What happened. We met every deadline except two, both of which, I should admit, I would probably have missed in a five-day week as well. Income over the six months was within three percent of the same period last year. Sick days fell from eleven to four. Most striking of all, nobody has applied for another job, which in a field where designers move constantly is not something I take for granted.
>
> What did not work. Thursdays became intense, sometimes unpleasantly so, as everyone tried to finish before the weekend. Two clients complained, politely but clearly, that we were harder to reach. Squeezing five days of work into four can be counterproductive if all it does is move the exhaustion from Friday to Thursday. And Sam, our illustrator, discovered that he preferred five shorter days to four long ones, because his best ideas tend to arrive slowly. A rule designed to give people more freedom had, for one of us, become a new kind of constraint.
>
> What we have decided. We will continue, with two changes. First, each person may choose either four longer days or five shorter ones, provided that the total is the same and that clients always know who to call. Second, no new work may be started on a Thursday afternoon, which should take some of the pressure off.
>
> A final thought. I do not believe that a four-day week is the answer for every business, and I am wary of anyone who says it is. What our experiment convinced me of is something narrower: that most of us had never seriously asked which of our hours were actually producing anything. As AI tools become more pervasive, that question seems likely to matter more each year, not less.

### Câu hỏi

**1. Why did Chloe read the results of the 2022 trial "with some suspicion"?**
*(Vì sao Chloe đọc kết quả thử nghiệm năm 2022 "với chút nghi ngờ"?)*

- A. because the companies had volunteered and most figures came from the companies themselves *(vì các công ty tự nguyện tham gia và phần lớn số liệu do chính họ báo cáo)*
- B. because the trial was very small, with only a few companies, and lasted just a few weeks *(vì cuộc thử nghiệm rất nhỏ, chỉ có vài công ty, và chỉ kéo dài vài tuần)*
- C. because Ruby had invented the article to persuade Chloe to try it *(vì Ruby bịa ra bài báo để thuyết phục Chloe thử)*
- D. because the companies had reduced pay, so the staff did not like the change *(vì các công ty đã giảm lương nên nhân viên không thích sự thay đổi)*

**2. What happened at the end of the UK trial?**
*(Cuối đợt thử nghiệm ở Anh, chuyện gì xảy ra?)*

- A. All companies stopped the trial and returned to a five-day week. *(Mọi công ty dừng thử nghiệm và quay lại tuần năm ngày.)*
- B. Pay was reduced, but most companies kept the shorter week. *(Lương bị giảm, nhưng đa số công ty vẫn giữ tuần ngắn hơn.)*
- C. Most companies continued, and 18 made the change permanent. *(Phần lớn công ty tiếp tục, và 18 công ty làm vĩnh viễn.)*
- D. Only five companies took part and all of them continued. *(Chỉ năm công ty tham gia và tất cả đều tiếp tục.)*

**3. What principle did the studio follow during the trial?**
*(Studio theo nguyên tắc nào trong đợt thử?)*

- A. Clients pay for the hours worked, so fewer hours cost less. *(Khách hàng trả tiền theo số giờ làm, nên ít giờ hơn thì rẻ hơn.)*
- B. Meetings are the most important work a studio does. *(Các cuộc họp là công việc quan trọng nhất của một studio.)*
- C. Fridays are kept free for clients only, by phone. *(Thứ Sáu chỉ dành cho khách hàng, qua điện thoại.)*
- D. Clients pay for the work done, not for the hours spent. *(Khách hàng trả tiền cho công việc đã làm, không phải cho số giờ bỏ ra.)*

**4. How did the studio make the shorter week possible?**
*(Studio làm thế nào để tuần ngắn hơn khả thi?)*

- A. by hiring more staff and giving each person fewer tasks to finish *(bằng cách thuê thêm người và giao mỗi người ít việc hơn để hoàn thành)*
- B. by cutting internal meetings and using AI tools for tedious tasks *(cắt họp nội bộ và dùng công cụ AI cho việc nhàm chán)*
- C. by refusing new clients and sending small jobs to freelancers *(bằng cách từ chối khách mới và giao việc nhỏ cho người làm tự do)*
- D. by asking everyone to work on weekends when a deadline was near *(bằng cách yêu cầu mọi người làm cuối tuần khi sắp đến hạn)*

**5. Why does Chloe admit that she "would probably have missed" the two deadlines anyway?**
*(Vì sao Chloe thừa nhận rằng hai lần trễ hạn "có lẽ đằng nào cũng trễ"?)*

- A. to be fair: the shorter week was probably not the cause of those delays *(để công bằng: tuần ngắn hơn có lẽ không phải nguyên nhân của hai lần trễ đó)*
- B. to blame her team for the delays while protecting the reputation of the studio *(để đổ lỗi cho nhóm về sự chậm trễ trong khi bảo vệ danh tiếng của studio)*
- C. to criticize the clients, who changed their minds so often *(để chê khách hàng, những người hay đổi ý quá)*
- D. to show that deadlines do not matter in a creative studio *(để cho thấy hạn chót không quan trọng ở một studio sáng tạo)*

**6. Which result does Chloe find "most striking of all"?**
*(Kết quả nào Chloe thấy "đáng chú ý nhất"?)*

- A. Income rose sharply, even though the team worked one day less. *(Thu nhập tăng mạnh, dù nhóm làm ít hơn một ngày.)*
- B. Clients stopped calling, so the team could work in peace. *(Khách hàng thôi gọi, nên nhóm làm việc yên ổn.)*
- C. Sick days rose, because everyone worked longer on Thursdays. *(Số ngày nghỉ ốm tăng, vì ai cũng làm lâu hơn vào thứ Năm.)*
- D. Nobody applied for another job, which is unusual in her field. *(Không ai nộp đơn đi chỗ khác, điều hiếm thấy trong ngành của cô.)*

**7. What problem did Sam's experience reveal?**
*(Trải nghiệm của Sam cho thấy vấn đề gì?)*

- A. He wanted to leave the studio and had already applied for a job in a bigger firm. *(Anh muốn rời studio và đã nộp đơn xin việc ở một công ty lớn hơn.)*
- B. A rule meant to give freedom could become a constraint for someone who works differently. *(Một quy định nhằm cho tự do lại có thể thành ràng buộc với người làm việc theo cách khác.)*
- C. He could not use AI tools, so he needed extra help from the others. *(Anh không dùng được công cụ AI nên cần người khác giúp thêm.)*
- D. He worked too slowly to meet deadlines, so the others finished his tasks. *(Anh làm quá chậm để kịp hạn, nên người khác hoàn thành việc của anh.)*

**8. What are the two changes the studio has decided to make?**
*(Hai thay đổi studio quyết định thực hiện là gì?)*

- A. returning to a five-day week for everyone and hiring a manager to check who is working *(quay lại tuần năm ngày cho mọi người và thuê một quản lý để kiểm tra ai đang làm việc)*
- B. cutting pay by ten percent and closing the studio completely on Thursdays *(giảm lương mười phần trăm và đóng hẳn studio vào thứ Năm)*
- C. letting people choose four longer or five shorter days, and no new work on Thursday afternoons *(cho mỗi người chọn bốn ngày dài hoặc năm ngày ngắn, và không nhận việc mới chiều thứ Năm)*
- D. working only with two clients and moving to a smaller office *(chỉ làm việc với hai khách hàng và chuyển sang văn phòng nhỏ hơn)*

## Bài đọc 2 (Unit 8) — Bài luận của Tracy khi chuyển về San Diego

> **Coming Home to a Place That Has Moved On**
>
> Tracy Carter, recorded for "Voices of Maple Street" and later written down at the library's request
>
> When my company opened an office in San Diego this summer and asked whether I would like to transfer, I said yes before the manager had finished the question. I had been away for six years, four at university and two at work, and I imagined coming home as a kind of return to the person I had been when I left.
>
> It took me about a week to realize how naive that was. My bedroom had become my brother's, and now, with him at university, it had become a room for guests, which is to say, for me. My cousin Leah, who used to live downstairs and answer my phone calls at midnight, is married and lives across town. My little sister, whom I last saw as a child who asked "Why?" about everything, is now taller than I am and asks far more difficult questions, mostly about why I left.
>
> The street, too, had moved on without consulting me. There is a park where there used to be a parking lot, a shared kitchen where there used to be a bakery, and neighbors I have never met who greet me politely, as one greets a visitor. At Harriet's, the coffee costs more than I remember, and I caught myself saying so in a tone that I recognized, with some horror, as exactly the tone in which old customers complain that the place "isn't the same". My parents, delighted to have me back, treated me for the first week with the slightly anxious politeness that people usually reserve for guests, which only made things worse.
>
> For a while I felt disparaging towards both versions of myself: the one who had left, for being so eager to go, and the one who had returned, for expecting everything to wait. What changed my mind was, of all things, a recording. The library asked me to interview Mrs. Harriet Brooks for its oral history project, and I listened beforehand to the recording my father had made of her earlier that year. In it, she describes arriving on Maple Street as a stranger with two suitcases, and becoming a neighbor not on the day she arrived but in the week of a flood, when everyone carried sandbags together. The library's note adds, gently, that the flood came two years later.
>
> I had been thinking of belonging as something you either keep or lose, like a key. Listening to her, I began to suspect that it is closer to a habit: something you build by showing up, again and again, for things that are not about you. It also echoed something I had learned at work, where respect is earned not by having been somewhere longest but by being reliable when it counts. Whatever I was when I left, I am not that person anymore, and the street is not that street. We will have to get to know each other again.
>
> So I have joined the park committee, I help my sister with her chemistry homework, and on Saturdays I interview neighbors for the library. Nobody greets me as a visitor anymore. Well, almost nobody: the dog still barks at me.

## Bài đọc 3 (Unit 7) — Tiểu luận: tuần làm mười lăm giờ chưa bao giờ đến

> **The Fifteen-Hour Week That Never Came**
>
> In 1930, at a moment when much of the world was sliding into economic depression, the economist John Maynard Keynes published a short essay that was, by the standards of the time, almost absurdly cheerful. Titled "Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren", it argued that the gloom of the moment was misleading, and that within a hundred years, steady growth and new technology would make people in the richer countries somewhere between four and eight times better off. So great would this abundance be, he suggested, that the old economic problem, the struggle for subsistence that had dominated human life for thousands of years, would be largely solved. The real challenge would then be how to fill our leisure, and he imagined that "three-hour shifts or a fifteen-hour week" might be enough to satisfy whatever need for work remained.
>
> Nearly a century later, the essay makes for strange reading. On the first prediction, Keynes was broadly right. By most measures, average incomes in the countries he had in mind have grown within the range he suggested, and machines he could only imagine now automate tasks, from complex computation to the sorting of mail, that once required whole floors of clerks. On the second, he was spectacularly wrong. Working hours fell for several decades and then, for many full-time employees, more or less stopped falling; in some professions they have risen again. The fifteen-hour week remains about as likely as the flying car.
>
> Why did the extra wealth not turn into extra time? Economists have offered several answers, none wholly satisfying on its own. One is that our wants turned out to be far more elastic than Keynes supposed. He distinguished between absolute needs, which can be satisfied, and the desire to feel superior to others, which cannot; he expected the first kind to dominate, but the second has proved remarkably durable. Each generation's luxuries become the next generation's necessities, and a house, a phone or a holiday that would have amazed our grandparents feels, to us, merely normal. A second answer is that much of the new wealth has been absorbed by things whose prices rose rather than fell, above all housing, healthcare and education, so that a family's income may be higher while the hours needed to afford a home are not lower.
>
> A third answer is less comfortable. For many people, work is no longer simply a means of earning a living; it has become a source of identity, standing and even friendship, and an hour removed from it can feel less like a gift than a loss. Keynes would perhaps not have been surprised; he worried that ordinary people, trained for so long to strive, might find themselves bereft of purpose once striving was no longer necessary.
>
> None of this means that the essay has failed. Its most lasting contribution may be the question it forces us to ask: if a society becomes rich enough to choose between more goods and more time, who decides which it chooses, and on what grounds? As automation becomes more pervasive, that question is unlikely to go away. Keynes thought that his grandchildren would answer it easily. We, his grandchildren, have mostly answered it by not asking it at all.

## Bài đọc 4 (Unit 7) — Blog của Nathan: một năm sau bài "ai sẽ đào tạo lớp kỹ sư mới?"

> **Who Will Train the Juniors? A Year Later**
>
> Nathan Brooks, from his blog about software and the people who write it
>
> Almost a year ago I published a piece called "Who Will Train the Juniors?", and several readers have asked how its plan has worked out. The honest answer is that I was half right: right about the problem, wrong about the cure.
>
> My proposal was that every junior should spend part of each week checking the AI's work and presenting what they found to a senior engineer, on the theory that you cannot judge an answer you do not understand. For about three months it looked like a success: the juniors flagged plenty of errors. Then a junior engineer whom I will call Rory asked me a question I have not stopped thinking about. He had just approved an AI-written fix to our system configuration in about four minutes, and instead of looking pleased, he looked worried. "I said it was fine because it looked like the fixes that are usually fine," he said. "If that's all I'm doing, how am I supposed to get good enough to do your job?"
>
> The theory was sound, but in practice it had been quietly turned around: unable to judge answers they did not understand, the juniors had learned to judge how an answer looked. Checking had become a kind of pattern-matching, which is precisely what the machine does, only more slowly. When I started out, there was no way to skip understanding: I spent months reading other people's code and writing tests for features I had not designed. It was tedious, and it felt almost menial, but it encoded something in my head that no course could have given me.
>
> To a company, a senior engineer with a good assistant looks like a small team for the price of one, and some firms have stopped hiring beginners altogether. Each decision makes sense on its own; taken together, they may prove deeply counterproductive. Experienced engineers do not appear from nowhere. A firm that pays nobody to be slow while they learn is quietly relying on other firms to do it instead.
>
> I still do not think the answer is to ban the tools, which would be both impossible and slightly ridiculous, like banning calculators in an accounting firm. What we changed in the spring was more modest: beginners may use any tool they like, but once a week they must explain to a colleague, without help, why their code actually works. Checking asks whether an answer looks right; explaining asks whether you know why it is right. We have also allotted a share of every project to work done by hand, slowly, with a senior engineer acting less as a supervisor than as an intermediary between the beginner and the machine. Our manager first described this as an expensive form of extracurricular activity, and he may have a point: it costs us time, and the benefit will not appear in this year's figures.
>
> I am wary of anyone who claims to know what this industry will look like in a decade, including myself, and a year ago I was more confident than I had any right to be. But I notice that the people who are best at using these tools are, almost without exception, the people who learned to work without them.
>
> As for Rory, he now runs our weekly explanation sessions, and he is ruthless. Last week he asked me to explain a piece of code I wrote six years ago. I could not.

## Bài đọc 5 (Unit 8) — Bài khoa học: bạn có còn là bạn sau sáu mươi năm?

> **Will You Still Be You?**
>
> What long-term studies suggest about the stability of the self
>
> In 1950, teachers in Scotland were asked to rate a large group of fourteen-year-olds on six qualities, including self-confidence, perseverance and originality. The ratings were filed away and, for the most part, forgotten. More than sixty years later, psychologists traced as many of those students as they could and asked 174 of them, by then aged about seventy-seven, along with a close friend or relative of each, to rate the same qualities again.
>
> Most people, asked to predict the result, expect a strong correlation. We like to think of character as something etched into us early, the stable core around which a life is built. The study found almost none. For most of the six qualities, there was no significant relationship between how a person had been described at fourteen and how they were described at seventy-seven. The authors were careful to point out the study's limits: each original rating came from a single teacher, the final sample was small, and many of the original participants could not be found. Even so, the result fits a large body of longitudinal research suggesting that personality changes gradually but substantially across a lifetime.
>
> If that is true, why does it feel so unlikely? A study published in 2013 offers part of an answer. Researchers asked more than nineteen thousand adults, aged eighteen to sixty-eight, either how much they had changed over the past ten years or how much they expected to change over the next ten. Eighteen-year-olds, for instance, predicted less change over the coming decade than twenty-eight-year-olds reported having experienced over the decade that had just ended. The same pattern appeared at every age, and the researchers gave it a name: the end of history illusion, the belief that the person you are now is, more or less, the finished version. It has practical consequences, too: in one experiment, people said they would pay considerably more to see their current favorite band in ten years' time than they would pay today to see the band they had loved ten years ago.
>
> Why should the illusion be so pervasive? One explanation is simply that remembering is easier than imagining. We can recall in detail how we used to be, but the person we will become is vague, and vagueness is easily mistaken for stability. Another is that believing in a finished self is comforting: it orients us, and it makes our choices feel like the expression of a settled identity rather than a bet placed on behalf of a stranger.
>
> None of this means that nothing about us lasts. Other research finds that a person's position relative to their peers, being more or less anxious than average, for example, tends to stay fairly stable over a few years. But the findings should make us a little more modest about two things: our certainty about who other people are, and our certainty about who we will be. The tattoo, the irrevocable decision, the career chosen at eighteen because it suits the person we are, are all promises made on behalf of someone we have not yet met. That person may be grateful, or may not; either way, it would be wise to leave them some room.

## Bài đọc 6 (Unit 8) — Chuyên mục của thầy Mitchell: hai mươi câu hỏi con gái tôi không muốn trả lời

> **Twenty Questions My Daughter Did Not Want to Answer**
>
> Mitchell Carter, history department, for the Westview High parents' newsletter
>
> Teaching at the school your own child attends has its hazards, and the greatest of them is the dinner table. Tina is the third of my children to endure this, and since September, when she started tenth grade and, worse, my history class, I have learned that any conversation about school will end with her asking me, politely but firmly, to stop being a history teacher at home. Last week I ignored that request, in the name of research, though the research is not mine.
>
> In the early 2000s, the psychologists Marshall Duke and Robyn Fivush, at Emory University, developed a simple questionnaire for children called the "Do You Know?" scale. It contains twenty questions, such as whether a child knows where their grandparents grew up, how their parents met, or what difficult times the family has been through. In their studies, children who knew more of the answers tended to report a stronger sense of control over their lives and higher self-esteem. As a history teacher, I should add at once that this is a correlation; families who tell stories may simply differ in other ways too, such as eating dinner together more often. The researchers were alert to this, and their explanation was not that knowing facts makes children happy, but that the stories give a child a sense of belonging to something larger and older than themselves.
>
> What interested me most was their finding about the kind of story that helps. Families, they suggested, tend to tell one of three kinds. In the rising kind, we started with nothing and worked our way up; in the falling kind, we once had everything and lost it. The third, which they called the oscillating story, is the most honest and, it seems, the most useful: we have had ups and downs, and whatever happened, we stuck together. A child who knows that kind of story learns something that a cocoon of praise cannot teach, namely that difficulty can be survived, and that it has been survived before by people with the same name.
>
> So I asked Tina the twenty questions. She answered six, eventually, and accused me of turning dinner into an exam. But one story caught her attention, and I realized, with some embarrassment, that I had never told it to her properly. My grandmother Dorothy became the family's main breadwinner in the 1950s, when my grandfather was ill for nearly two years. She had no car, so on the morning of her interview for a bookkeeping job across town she hitched a ride with the milkman and was hired, she always claimed, because she was the only applicant who checked the shop's accounts for mistakes while waiting. Whether every detail is true, I have no idea; my aunt tells it with a different milkman. Tina listened with an expression I have not seen since she was seven, and then said, with deep suspicion, "Is this going to be on a test?"
>
> It is not, but I have come to think that the most important things we teach our children may not be lessons at all. They are the stories we tell, half by accident, about the people who came before them, and especially about the times those people stumbled and kept going. Try the twenty questions at your own table, and expect some eye-rolling; in my experience, it is a sign that the stories are being heard.

## 💡 Mẹo làm bài

- Báo cáo C2 hay **tự nêu hạn chế của nguồn** (`volunteered`, `reported by the companies themselves`). Câu hỏi "why with suspicion" lấy ngay câu đó.
- Kết quả được trình bày trung thực (`I should admit`) là chỗ hỏi **thái độ người viết**: công bằng, không phóng đại.
- Bài luận tự sự: theo dõi **ý niệm trước → sự kiện làm đổi ý → ý niệm sau**. Ẩn dụ (`like a key` ↔ `a habit`) thường là đáp án câu hỏi "how does … change".
- Chi tiết nhắc lại chương trước (giọng than "isn't the same", trận lụt, bao cát) là gợi ý cho câu suy luận liên chương.
- Tiểu luận đánh giá dự đoán: tách **từng dự đoán** và **phán xét của tác giả cho từng cái** (`broadly right` ↔ `spectacularly wrong`). Phương án gộp "đúng cả hai" / "sai cả hai" là bẫy.
- Bài blog có lập luận: câu ngắn sau câu dài (`Experienced engineers do not appear from nowhere.`) thường là ý chính của đoạn; câu ngay sau giải thích nó.
- Bài khoa học C2: tác giả nêu **giới hạn nghiên cứu** rồi dùng `Even so` để giữ kết luận. Câu hỏi "why list the limits" → thận trọng, không phải bác bỏ.
- Bài tự sự có số liệu: người viết tự chặn suy diễn quá đà (`this is a correlation`). Nối với bài Leah ở chương 5–6: tương quan ≠ nhân quả.
