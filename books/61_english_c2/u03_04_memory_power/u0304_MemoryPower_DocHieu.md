## Bài đọc 1 (Unit 3) — Mitchell: khi kho lưu trữ không khớp lời kể của hàng xóm

> **When the Archive Disagrees with My Neighbor**
>
> Mitchell Carter, volunteer interviewer for the Pine Street Library oral history project "Voices of Maple Street"
>
> My interview with Harriet Brooks was to have taken an hour. It took three afternoons, four pots of coffee and, by the end, a small disagreement between my neighbor and the city's newspaper archive that I have been turning over in my mind ever since.
>
> Harriet remembers arriving on Maple Street in 1978, a young teacher with two suitcases, in the same week as the great storm, when the old fire station on Pine Street flooded and the firefighters rowed a boat down the middle of the street. She told the story vividly, with the confidence of someone who has told it many times. The archive tells a different story. The flood happened, and there is even a photograph of the boat, but it happened in 1980, two years after she moved in.
>
> My first reaction, I am ashamed to say, was the historian's small thrill of catching a witness in an error. My second was to wonder why the error had taken that particular shape. It would have been easy to chalk the mistake up to the passing of time, correct the date quietly and move on.
>
> Psychologists have studied such errors for decades. On the morning after the space shuttle Challenger exploded in January 1986, the psychologists Ulric Neisser and Nicole Harsch asked a group of university students to write down exactly how they had heard the news. More than two years later, they asked the same students again. Many of the new accounts differed markedly from the originals, yet the students were highly confident, and their confidence bore no relation to their accuracy. Some, it has been reported, when shown the pages in their own handwriting, preferred the later memory.
>
> In hindsight, Harriet's error looks less like carelessness than like meaning. For her, both events stood for the same thing: the moment when a stranger became a neighbor. Had it not been for the flood, she told me, she might never have met half the street, because everyone spent that week carrying sandbags together. Over time, memory had quietly moved the flood closer to her arrival, where it belonged emotionally if not chronologically. The archival record is not wrong, and neither, in its own way, is Harriet. The task is not to choose between them but to reconcile them, because memories are ephemeral in their details and stubborn in their meaning, whereas dates are exactly the opposite.
>
> So which version should the archive keep for posterity? Both, I think, and side by side. The newspaper gives us the date; Harriet gives us what the date meant. An oral history that has been corrected until it agrees with the documents is no longer an oral history; an archive without memories is merely a list of things that happened to nobody in particular.
>
> When I showed Harriet the photograph of the boat, she studied it for a long time. "Well," she said at last, "I would have liked to have been there. I suppose, in a way, I was."

### Câu hỏi

**1. What does Mitchell mean when he says the interview "was to have taken an hour"?**
*(Mitchell muốn nói gì khi bảo cuộc phỏng vấn "lẽ ra chỉ mất một giờ"?)*

- A. It was cancelled because Harriet was too busy to continue. *(Buổi phỏng vấn bị hủy vì Harriet quá bận để tiếp tục.)*
- B. It took exactly one hour, as Harriet had promised him. *(Nó kéo dài đúng một giờ, như Harriet đã hứa với ông.)*
- C. It had been planned to last an hour but took much longer. *(Nó được dự định kéo dài một giờ nhưng thực tế lâu hơn nhiều.)*
- D. Harriet refused to be interviewed after the first afternoon. *(Harriet từ chối được phỏng vấn sau buổi chiều đầu tiên.)*

**2. According to the archive, what was wrong with Harriet's story?**
*(Theo kho lưu trữ, câu chuyện của bà Harriet sai ở đâu?)*

- A. The flood happened two years after she arrived, not in the week she arrived. *(Trận lụt xảy ra hai năm sau khi bà tới, không phải đúng tuần bà tới.)*
- B. There was never a flood on Pine Street, only a heavy storm that nobody remembers well. *(Chưa từng có lũ ở phố Pine, chỉ có một cơn bão lớn mà không ai nhớ rõ.)*
- C. The fire station did not exist yet when she arrived on Maple Street. *(Trạm cứu hỏa chưa tồn tại khi bà đến phố Maple.)*
- D. She arrived in 1980, two years later than she remembers. *(Bà đến vào năm 1980, muộn hơn hai năm so với bà nhớ.)*

**3. Why does Mitchell say he is "ashamed" of his first reaction?**
*(Vì sao Mitchell nói ông "xấu hổ" về phản ứng đầu tiên?)*

- A. because he had lost the photograph of the boat that Harriet had asked to see again *(vì ông đã làm mất tấm ảnh chiếc thuyền mà Harriet muốn xem lại)*
- B. because he had been rude to Harriet when she told him the story of the storm *(vì ông đã thô lỗ với Harriet khi bà kể câu chuyện về cơn bão)*
- C. because he had made the same mistake himself in the interview with Harriet *(vì chính ông đã mắc cùng lỗi đó trong buổi phỏng vấn Harriet)*
- D. because he felt pleased at catching his neighbor in an error instead of asking why it happened *(vì ông thấy thích thú khi bắt lỗi người hàng xóm thay vì hỏi vì sao lỗi xảy ra)*

**4. What did Neisser and Harsch find when they asked the students again?**
*(Neisser và Harsch phát hiện gì khi hỏi lại các sinh viên?)*

- A. Most students remembered the news perfectly after more than two years. *(Hầu hết sinh viên nhớ tin đó hoàn hảo sau hơn hai năm.)*
- B. Many accounts had changed, but confident students were not more accurate. *(Nhiều lời kể đã thay đổi, nhưng sinh viên tự tin không chính xác hơn.)*
- C. The students refused to answer because they had forgotten the event. *(Sinh viên từ chối trả lời vì họ đã quên sự kiện.)*
- D. Only confident students made mistakes, while the careful ones remembered well. *(Chỉ sinh viên tự tin mới sai, còn người cẩn thận nhớ tốt.)*

**5. What is most surprising about the students who were shown their own handwriting?**
*(Điều đáng ngạc nhiên nhất về những sinh viên được xem chữ viết tay của chính mình là gì?)*

- A. They could not read their own handwriting, so they asked the researchers to read it. *(Họ không đọc được chữ viết tay của chính mình nên nhờ các nhà nghiên cứu đọc hộ.)*
- B. They thanked the researchers for showing them the old pages. *(Họ cảm ơn các nhà nghiên cứu đã cho xem những trang cũ.)*
- C. Some trusted their later memory more than their own written record. *(Có người tin ký ức về sau hơn cả bản ghi chính tay mình viết.)*
- D. They all corrected their stories as soon as they saw the pages. *(Tất cả họ sửa lại lời kể ngay khi thấy các trang giấy.)*

**6. Why does Mitchell describe the study of the Challenger memories?**
*(Vì sao Mitchell kể về nghiên cứu ký ức vụ Challenger?)*

- A. to show that Harriet's kind of error is common and does not mean she is careless *(để cho thấy lỗi như của Harriet là phổ biến và không có nghĩa bà cẩu thả)*
- B. to prove that the archive is always wrong when it disagrees with people who remember *(để chứng minh kho lưu trữ luôn sai khi nó mâu thuẫn với người còn nhớ sự việc)*
- C. to explain space travel and how students heard about the Challenger launch *(để giải thích du hành vũ trụ và cách sinh viên biết về vụ phóng Challenger)*
- D. to criticize university students for forgetting something so important *(để chê sinh viên đại học vì quên một chuyện quan trọng đến vậy)*

**7. According to Mitchell, why did Harriet's memory move the flood closer to her arrival?**
*(Theo Mitchell, vì sao ký ức của bà Harriet dời trận lụt lại gần ngày bà tới?)*

- A. because she was simply confused about the years and had never checked them *(vì bà chỉ đơn giản nhầm các năm và chưa bao giờ kiểm tra lại)*
- B. because a newspaper had reported the date wrongly at the time *(vì một tờ báo đã đưa sai ngày vào thời điểm đó)*
- C. because she wanted to impress Mitchell and the library with a more dramatic story of arriving *(vì bà muốn gây ấn tượng với Mitchell và thư viện bằng một câu chuyện đến phố kịch tính hơn)*
- D. because both events meant the same thing to her: becoming part of the neighborhood *(vì với bà, cả hai sự kiện cùng mang một ý nghĩa: trở thành một phần của khu phố)*

**8. What does "where it belonged emotionally if not chronologically" mean?**
*(Cụm "nơi nó thuộc về về mặt cảm xúc dù không phải về mặt niên đại" nghĩa là gì?)*

- A. The flood should be moved to a different year in the library's archive. *(Trận lũ nên được chuyển sang một năm khác trong kho lưu trữ của thư viện.)*
- B. In her feelings the flood belonged to her arrival, although the dates differ. *(Trong cảm xúc của bà, trận lũ thuộc về lúc bà đến, dù ngày tháng khác nhau.)*
- C. The flood happened twice, once for her feelings and once in the archive. *(Trận lũ xảy ra hai lần, một lần trong cảm xúc của bà và một lần trong kho lưu trữ.)*
- D. Her emotions were wrong, so her memory of the dates could not be trusted. *(Cảm xúc của bà sai, nên không thể tin trí nhớ của bà về ngày tháng.)*

**9. Which version does Mitchell think the archive should keep?**

## Bài đọc 3 (Unit 3) — Tiểu luận khoa học: số phận kỳ lạ của nỗi hoài niệm

> **The Strange Career of Nostalgia**
>
> For two centuries it was treated as a disease, and psychologists now suspect that it may be closer to a cure.
>
> In 1688 a young medical student in the Swiss city of Basel, Johannes Hofer, wrote a thesis about an illness that he believed had never been properly described. Those who had it, mostly young Swiss people living and working far from home, lost their appetite, slept badly, cried without warning and, in the worst cases, were said to waste away entirely. Hofer named the condition by joining two Greek words, nostos, meaning a return home, and algos, meaning pain, and so the word nostalgia entered the language as a medical diagnosis. For the next two centuries doctors took it seriously, prescribing leeches, opium or, more reasonably, a journey home.
>
> In hindsight it is tempting to chalk such treatments up to the ignorance of the period, but the assumption behind them survived far longer than the leeches did. Well into the twentieth century, nostalgia was regarded as a form of melancholy, a sign that the sufferer could not cope with the present, and a nostalgic person was seen at best as sentimental and at worst as unwell. Only in recent decades has this view begun to wane.
>
> Much of the credit belongs to a group of psychologists at the University of Southampton in England, among them Constantine Sedikides and Tim Wildschut, who began around the turn of the century to study nostalgia not as a symptom but as an ordinary emotion. When people are asked to recall a nostalgic memory, they usually describe a scene full of other people, such as a birthday, a wedding or a summer vacation, and they tend to come away feeling more connected to others and more confident that their lives have meaning. Oddly, they also report feeling more optimistic about the future. Loneliness, it turns out, makes people more nostalgic, and nostalgia in turn eases loneliness, as if the mind kept its own recourse against isolation. In one memorable study, people were more likely to feel nostalgic on cold days, and those who had been asked to recall a nostalgic memory said that they felt physically warmer.
>
> None of this means that nostalgia is always harmless. Researchers distinguish between the kind that looks back with gratitude and the kind that puts the past on a pedestal in order to condemn the present, and only the first seems to do people much good. Memory also edits as it preserves, smoothing the ebb and flow of ordinary days into a story with a shape. The past we feel nostalgic about is, in this sense, partly a work of fiction, which may be exactly why it comforts us.
>
> There is a pleasing irony in the history of the word. The emotion that Hofer believed could kill his patients is now being studied as a way of helping students settle into university and helping older people cope with loss. Had it not been for two centuries of medical error, we might have recognized much sooner what most people already knew, which is that looking back is one of the ways we find the strength to go on. The lesson for posterity is a modest one: before we try to cure a feeling, we should first find out what it is for.

## Bài đọc 4 (Unit 3) — Trích hồi ký: hai anh em và quầy hàng chia đôi

> **Two Brothers, One Counter**
>
> From The Store on Elm Street, a memoir by Joanna Gallagher
>
> For twenty-three years my grandfather and his brother ran a hardware store in a small town in Ohio without speaking a single word to each other. In family legend, which grew more fabled with every telling, the rift began in the spring of 1971 over a delivery of paint. My grandfather, Frank, had ordered forty cans of a green that his brother Leonard considered an insult to the store, and by the end of the afternoon a line of tape had been laid down the middle of the floor.
>
> What followed was less a quarrel than a long, silent arrangement. Frank served the customers on the left side of the counter and Leonard those on the right, and each kept his own cash register, his own coffee pot and his own opinion of the other. Customers learned the rules quickly, and a man who needed nails from the right side and a hammer from the left simply paid twice and said nothing. Messages between the brothers passed through my mother, who was ten when it began and who spent her teenage years carrying notes across eight feet of floor, a role she later described as "mediation with no pay and no hope of a result." Several attempts at reconciliation came to nothing. A conciliatory Christmas card from Leonard was returned without being opened, and when my grandmother seated the brothers together at my parents' wedding, Frank turned to the oldest tactic of all and left before dessert.
>
> The arrangement ended only in 1994, when Leonard's health failed and the store was sold. It was not until last year, when I began sorting the boxes of papers that my mother had kept in her basement, that I discovered how little I had understood. Among the extant records were the store's account books, kept separately by each brother in his own handwriting, and they tell a story that no one in the family had ever heard.
>
> In the winter of 1982, when a large chain store opened on the edge of town and business on Leonard's side fell by nearly half, someone began paying his suppliers. The payments appear in Frank's book without explanation, every month for almost two years, under a single word: "Expenses." Three years later, when Frank was ill and could not work for a whole summer, the same thing appears in Leonard's book in reverse, under the same word. Neither man, as far as I can tell, ever told the other, and neither ever said thank you.
>
> I have tried, since then, to reconcile the brothers in my memory with the brothers in the archival record, and I am not sure that it can be done. In hindsight the tape on the floor looks less like a fracture than a strange kind of edifice, a structure that two proud men built so that they could go on caring for each other without ever having to admit it. When I showed my mother the books, she read them for a long time and then said only that it was "exactly like them", which I took to be both a rebuke and a kind of praise. I still do not know who was right about the paint, and I suspect that, by the end, neither of them did either.

## Bài đọc 5 (Unit 4) — Bài tổng quan nghiên cứu: quyền lực làm tha hoá hay làm lộ diện?

> **Does Power Corrupt, or Does It Reveal?**
>
> A review of the psychological evidence
>
> The idea that power corrupts is one of the oldest in moral thought, and it is usually stated as though it needed no evidence at all. Over the past three decades, however, social psychologists have tried to test it in the laboratory, and the results are both more troubling and more hopeful than the proverb suggests.
>
> Some of the most quoted findings come from Dacher Keltner, an eminent psychologist at the University of California, Berkeley. In one early study, students were placed in groups of three to discuss a set of proposals for their university, and one member of each group was given, at random, the task of judging the others' ideas. In the middle of the discussion, a plate of five cookies was brought in. Most groups politely left the last cookie on the plate, but the fourth was more likely to be taken by the person who had been put in charge, who was also more likely to eat it with an open mouth and scatter crumbs across the table. Nothing about these students had changed except a role assigned by chance, which they had held for only a short time.
>
> A study published in 2006 by Adam Galinsky and his colleagues used an even more basic test. Some participants were asked to recall a time when they had held power over someone, others a time when someone had held power over them, and all were then asked to draw a capital letter E on their own foreheads. Those in the high-power group were almost three times as likely to draw the letter so that it read correctly to themselves and backwards to anyone looking at them. The researchers saw this as a small sign of a large problem, since power seems to hamper our ability to see things from another person's point of view.
>
> It would be easy to stop there, but the picture begins to unravel when one looks more closely. In 2001 the psychologist Serena Chen and her colleagues found that power did not affect everyone in the same way. People who thought of their relationships mainly in terms of caring for others became more generous, not less, when they were given authority, whereas those focused on their own interests became more selfish. Power, on this account, does not so much corrupt people as release them, revealing intentions that were already there but had been held in check. A familiar caution also applies, because some experiments in social psychology have proved harder to repeat than the headlines suggested, and a careful reader should treat any single study as a hint rather than a verdict.
>
> If the revealing view is right, the practical lesson changes. Organizations cannot rely on finding leaders who are immune to temptation, since nobody is, but they can choose people whose habits already point outward and surround them with accountability rather than admiration. Keltner has argued that people tend to gain power through precisely the qualities, such as generosity and attention to others, that power later wears away, a pattern he calls the power paradox. Stringent rules can act as a deterrent, but perhaps the most basic safeguard is also the oldest: make sure that the person at the helm is regularly told, by someone who has nothing to fear, how things look from the other side of the table.

## Bài đọc 6 (Unit 4) — Chuyên mục châm biếm của Nathan: cẩm nang quyền lực chốn văn phòng

> **A Field Guide to Office Power**
>
> Nathan Brooks, for the Maple Street Neighbors' Newsletter, April
>
> When I became the leader of a small team of software engineers, several people who meant well told me that I would now need to learn the rules of office power that nobody writes down. Nobody could tell me what these rules were, so I have spent almost two years watching closely, and I offer my findings here as a public service, for the sake of anyone who is about to be promoted against their better judgment.
>
> The first rule is that no important decision is ever made in the meeting where it is officially made. It is made the day before, over coffee, by the three people who arrived early, and the meeting itself exists so that everyone else can find out what they have already agreed to. The skilled operator never argues in public, but surreptitiously solicits "a quick word" with each person beforehand, so that by the time he speaks, the outcome is no longer in doubt.
>
> The second rule concerns urgency, which can be fabricated in almost unlimited quantities. An email marked "time-sensitive" that arrives at six in the evening is rarely about time and almost always about power, because it announces that the sender's priorities have now become yours. I once watched a manager coax an entire department into working on a Saturday for a deadline that turned out to be entirely bogus, and he was praised afterward for his leadership.
>
> The third rule is that credit, unlike blame, flows upward. When a project succeeds, the person at the helm describes it, with genuine modesty, as a team effort, and then presents it to senior management alone. When a project fails, the same modesty disappears, and it is discovered that a hapless junior engineer was responsible all along.
>
> The fourth rule is that information is money. Every office has its informant, the friendly colleague who will tell you what is being said about you and will tell others, with equal friendliness, what you said in reply. Gossip of this kind is rife in teams that are insular and anxious, and almost absent in teams where people are told the truth directly, which tells you something about where it comes from.
>
> I should admit that I have broken most of these rules, not out of virtue but out of incompetence. I cannot remember who said what before meetings, I mark nothing as urgent because I forget to, and when something goes wrong it is usually, in fact, my fault. Oddly, this has worked rather well, since my team argues with me openly in meetings, which is unnerving and extremely useful. If there is a lesson here, it is perhaps that most office power games are played in self-defense, and that a leader who refuses to play them removes the reason for everyone else to play them too. My mother, who read a draft of this column, says that I have simply described what any good elementary school teacher knows by the age of thirty, and that I could have saved myself almost two years by asking her.

## 💡 Mẹo làm bài

- Bài luận có **hai phản ứng** của người viết (`My first reaction… My second…`). Câu hỏi thái độ hỏi vì sao người viết bác bỏ phản ứng đầu.
- Cấu trúc `X if not Y` (`emotionally if not chronologically`) = đúng theo X, dù không đúng theo Y.
- Truyện C2 ít khi có kẻ xấu rõ ràng. Câu hỏi về nhân vật (`Helena`, `Mr. Pell`) thường xoay quanh **cách họ cư xử**, không phải ai đúng ai sai.
- Chi tiết cuối truyện (tấm biển trên cổng) thường **trả lời lại** xung đột chính. Đối chiếu với điều kiện ban đầu (tên, giờ đóng cửa).
- Tiểu luận khoa học hay mở bằng một **chi tiết lịch sử** rồi lật lại ("pleasing irony"): câu hỏi mục đích đoạn thường hỏi chi tiết ấy dùng để làm gì.
- Câu hỏi NOT/EXCEPT: đánh dấu từng phương án có trong bài, phương án còn lại (không có hoặc ngược nghĩa) là đáp án.
- Bài châm biếm nói NGƯỢC điều mình nghĩ ("as a public service", "not out of virtue but out of incompetence"): chọn phương án nói đúng ý ngầm, không chọn nghĩa đen.
- Chuyên mục châm biếm: "luật" được trình bày như lời khuyên nhưng thực ra là lời chê. Câu hỏi "ironic / with some irony" hỏi khoảng cách giữa điều nói ra và điều người viết nghĩ.
- Bài tổng quan nghiên cứu hay lật lại kết luận bằng `does not so much X as Y` hoặc `the picture begins to unravel`. Ý chính nằm ở vế sau.
