## Bài đọc 1 (Unit 1) — Bài luận của Megan về "bạn giả" và lời nói giảm

> **False Friends and Gentle Words**
>
> Megan Carter, emergency nurse, for the anthology of the Thursday evening Spanish class at City College
>
> Three years ago, in my first month of Spanish classes, I tried to apologize to the daughter of a patient for keeping her waiting. I wanted to say that I was embarrassed. What I actually said, with great sincerity, was that I was very pregnant. The Spanish word "embarazada" looks as if it should mean "embarrassed", but it denotes something rather different. The daughter stared at me, then laughed until she cried, and the tension in the room simply disappeared.
>
> Teachers call such words "false friends", and every learner collects a few painful ones. But I have come to believe that they are the easy part. A false friend announces itself sooner or later, usually with laughter. The harder problem lies in words whose dictionary meaning is perfectly correct, but whose connotation leads the listener somewhere the speaker never intended to go.
>
> Hospital English is full of them. We say that a patient is receiving "comfort care", that we will "keep him comfortable", that his numbers are "not where we would like them to be". Every one of these phrases is true, and every one of them is kindly meant. Ostensibly, such phrases exist to spare the family pain, and sometimes they do. Yet a frightened family, whilst listening to them, will often hear a promise of recovery that nobody has made. The euphemism protects the speaker at least as much as the listener.
>
> Our jargon causes similar trouble. When a doctor writes that a patient is "a poor historian", it simply means that he cannot give a clear account of his illness. One patient's son read it in a report and was deeply hurt, convinced that we had judged his father's intelligence. Colloquial English can be just as confusing for patients who learned the language from textbooks. A man from Guatemala once asked me, quite reasonably, why the doctor had told him to "hang in there", and where exactly "there" was.
>
> Early in my career, a senior nurse gave me a rule that I have tried to follow ever since. When a patient is dying, say the word "dying" once, clearly and unequivocally, and then be as gentle as you like. A soft word is no more a kindness than a locked door is a welcome, if the person outside cannot find the handle.
>
> Some colleagues find this rule harsh, and I understand why. Be that as it may, I have never once had a family thank me for a gentle phrase that they misunderstood, whereas many have thanked me, sometimes years later, for telling them the truth while there was still time to say goodbye.
>
> Learning Spanish has not made me fluent; my teacher would describe my grammar as "creative". What it has done is make me listen differently, in both languages, for the distance between what a sentence says and what it lets someone believe.

### Câu hỏi

**1. What happened when Megan tried to apologize in Spanish?**
*(Chuyện gì xảy ra khi Megan cố xin lỗi bằng tiếng Tây Ban Nha?)*

- A. The daughter was offended by the word she used and asked another nurse to help instead. *(Cô con gái phật ý vì từ Megan dùng và nhờ một y tá khác giúp thay thế.)*
- B. She used a word that means "pregnant" instead of "embarrassed", and the daughter laughed. *(Cô dùng một từ nghĩa là "có thai" thay vì "ngượng", và cô con gái bật cười.)*
- C. Her teacher corrected her in front of the whole class the next day. *(Cô giáo sửa lỗi cho cô trước cả lớp vào hôm sau.)*
- D. The patient could not understand her accent and asked her to write the word down. *(Bệnh nhân không hiểu giọng của cô và đề nghị cô viết từ đó ra.)*

**2. Why does Megan say that false friends are "the easy part"?**
*(Vì sao Megan nói "bạn giả" là "phần dễ"?)*

- A. because there are very few of them and every learner knows them already *(vì chúng rất ít và người học nào cũng đã biết rồi)*
- B. because teachers explain them all in the first class, before any mistakes are made *(vì giáo viên giải thích hết chúng ngay buổi đầu, trước khi ai mắc lỗi)*
- C. because they only exist in Spanish, so nurses seldom meet them at work *(vì chúng chỉ có trong tiếng Tây Ban Nha, nên y tá hiếm khi gặp ở nơi làm việc)*
- D. because the mistake soon becomes obvious, while misleading connotations may be missed *(vì lỗi sớm muộn cũng lộ ra, còn sắc thái gây hiểu lầm có thể bị bỏ sót)*

**3. What point does Megan make about hospital phrases like "comfort care"?**
*(Megan nêu ý gì về những cụm như "comfort care" trong bệnh viện?)*

- A. They are accurate and kind, but frightened families may hear hope that was never offered. *(Chúng chính xác và tử tế, nhưng gia đình đang sợ hãi có thể nghe ra hy vọng chưa từng được hứa.)*
- B. They are medically wrong, and families are right to complain about them. *(Chúng sai về mặt y khoa, và các gia đình có lý khi phàn nàn về chúng.)*
- C. They should be translated into Spanish so that every patient can understand them. *(Chúng nên được dịch sang tiếng Tây Ban Nha để bệnh nhân nào cũng hiểu.)*
- D. Doctors use them to save time, even though families would prefer longer explanations. *(Bác sĩ dùng chúng để tiết kiệm thời gian, dù gia đình thích giải thích dài hơn.)*

**4. What does Megan imply by "The euphemism protects the speaker at least as much as the listener"?**
*(Megan hàm ý gì qua câu "Lời nói giảm bảo vệ người nói ít nhất cũng nhiều như bảo vệ người nghe"?)*

- A. Speakers are often in danger, so they choose their words carefully for safety. *(Người nói thường gặp nguy hiểm, nên chọn lời cẩn thận để an toàn.)*
- B. Euphemisms are required by hospital rules, and senior nurses check that staff use them. *(Nói giảm là quy định của bệnh viện, và các y tá trưởng kiểm tra nhân viên có dùng không.)*
- C. Soft phrases also spare staff the discomfort of saying something painful directly. *(Những cụm nói giảm cũng giúp nhân viên tránh nỗi khó chịu khi phải nói thẳng điều đau lòng.)*
- D. Listeners prefer direct words, and speakers protect them by avoiding euphemisms. *(Người nghe thích lời nói thẳng, và người nói bảo vệ họ bằng cách tránh nói giảm.)*

**5. Why was the patient's son hurt by the phrase "a poor historian"?**
*(Vì sao con trai bệnh nhân bị tổn thương bởi cụm "a poor historian"?)*

- A. He misunderstood medical jargon as a judgment of his father's intelligence. *(Anh hiểu nhầm thuật ngữ y khoa thành lời đánh giá trí tuệ của bố mình.)*
- B. His father was a history teacher and expected the report to be kinder. *(Cha anh là giáo viên lịch sử và mong bản báo cáo tử tế hơn.)*
- C. The doctor had insulted him in person, and the report repeated the insult in writing. *(Bác sĩ đã xúc phạm anh trực tiếp, và bản báo cáo lặp lại lời xúc phạm bằng chữ viết.)*
- D. The report contained false information about his father's illness and age. *(Bản báo cáo chứa thông tin sai về bệnh và tuổi của cha anh.)*

**6. What is the meaning of the rule Megan learned from a senior nurse?**
*(Quy tắc Megan học từ điều dưỡng lâu năm có nghĩa gì?)*

- A. Never talk about death with families, because it only makes them more afraid. *(Đừng bao giờ nói về cái chết với gia đình, vì điều đó chỉ làm họ sợ hơn.)*
- B. State the hard truth plainly once, then offer as much gentleness as needed. *(Nói rõ sự thật khó nghe một lần, rồi dịu dàng bao nhiêu cũng được.)*
- C. Always use medical words, so that families do not misunderstand the doctors. *(Luôn dùng từ y khoa, để gia đình không hiểu nhầm các bác sĩ.)*
- D. Let doctors speak instead of nurses, because families trust them more. *(Để bác sĩ nói thay y tá, vì gia đình tin họ hơn.)*

**7. What does the comparison with "a locked door" suggest?**
*(Phép so sánh với "một cánh cửa khoá" gợi ý điều gì?)*

- A. Hospitals should keep their doors open at all hours so that families can visit at any time. *(Bệnh viện nên mở cửa mọi giờ để gia đình có thể thăm bất cứ lúc nào.)*
- B. Families should not enter patients' rooms because they only make things harder. *(Người nhà không nên vào phòng bệnh vì họ chỉ làm mọi việc khó hơn.)*
- C. Gentle words are always cruel, because they hide the truth from the patient. *(Lời nhẹ nhàng luôn tàn nhẫn, vì chúng giấu sự thật khỏi bệnh nhân.)*
- D. A gentle phrase is not truly kind if it stops the listener from reaching the truth. *(Một câu nói nhẹ không thật sự tử tế nếu nó khiến người nghe không chạm được tới sự thật.)*

**8. How does Megan respond to colleagues who find her rule harsh?**
*(Megan đáp lại thế nào với đồng nghiệp thấy quy tắc của cô khắc nghiệt?)*

- A. She agrees with them and has stopped using the rule in her own work. *(Cô đồng ý với họ và đã thôi áp dụng quy tắc đó trong công việc.)*
- B. She says they are not real nurses and refuses to talk to them about it. *(Cô nói họ không phải y tá thật sự và từ chối bàn với họ về chuyện đó.)*
- C. She accepts their feeling but points to families' gratitude for the truth. *(Cô chấp nhận cảm giác của họ nhưng chỉ ra rằng các gia đình biết ơn sự thật.)*
- D. She asks the hospital to change its rules so that nobody has to say it. *(Cô đề nghị bệnh viện đổi quy định để không ai phải nói ra điều đó.)*

## Bài đọc 2 (Unit 2) — Bài luận của bà Harriet: quán còn là "Harriet's" không?

> **Is It Still My Café?**
>
> Harriet Brooks, for the Maple Street Neighbors' Newsletter, January
>
> Last week, a customer who has been coming to Harriet's since the year it opened put down her cup and announced, with some sadness, that the place "isn't really the same anymore". I have been thinking about her remark ever since, partly because she is right and partly because she is wrong.
>
> Consider what has changed. The walls my son painted yellow have been repainted twice. The heart-shaped sign was designed by a waitress who now runs her own studio. We take payment on a tablet, the coffee costs more than I care to admit, and next door there is a kitchen full of cooks I had never met a year ago. Of the people who served the first cup of coffee, only one is still here, and she now needs a stool behind the counter.
>
> The ancient Greeks had a puzzle for this. According to the writer Plutarch, the people of Athens kept the ship of their hero Theseus for many years, replacing each rotten plank with a new one, until philosophers began to ask whether it was still the same ship. Centuries later, the English philosopher Thomas Hobbes made the puzzle crueler. Suppose, he said, someone had collected all the old planks and built a second ship from them. Which of the two would be the real one?
>
> Hobbes's question is not as abstract as it sounds. Our old chairs are in the shared kitchen, and the old sign hangs in my son's garage. If you put them together in an empty room, would you have Harriet's? I am quite sure you would not, and the reason tells me something.
>
> What makes a place itself is not its planks but its habits: the soup on Wednesdays, the coffee kept warm for the nurse who comes off the night shift, the fact that whoever is behind the counter will ask how your mother is and actually wait for the answer. If there is an axiom in my trade, it is that people come back not for the coffee but for the feeling of being expected. Its authenticity, if that is the right word, is intrinsic to what people do there, not to the objects they do it with.
>
> I should also confess a small fallacy in my customer's sadness, which I share on bad days. We remember the old café as it felt, not as it was. I remember the leaking roof, the broken heater and the winter when we nearly closed. Were it not for the changes she regrets, there would be no café for her to feel nostalgic about; it would have closed, as the bakery did.
>
> One day, someone else will run this place, and they will change things I cannot imagine. So be it. As long as the person behind the counter still waits for the answer, it will still be Harriet's, whether or not there is a Harriet in it.

## Bài đọc 3 (Unit 1) — Bài điểm sách: cuốn cẩm nang "tiếng Anh chuẩn" của Edwin Garrity

> **The Last Defender of the Rules**
>
> Mind Your Language, by Edwin Garrity — reviewed by Caroline Ashby
>
> The blurb on the back of Edwin Garrity's new book promises a lively defense of correct English in an age of careless speech, which is a fair description, provided that one does not place too much weight on the word "lively". Garrity, a retired university teacher, has written a prescriptive guide in the oldest sense: he does not wish to describe how English is used, but to stipulate how it ought to be used. He drones on about split infinitives, sentences that end with prepositions and the sad decline of the word "whom", and on almost every page the reader is assured that standards are falling.
>
> It is ironic that a book so concerned with the past should know so little about it. The rule against ending a sentence with a preposition appears to have begun with the poet John Dryden, who in 1672 criticized a line by Ben Jonson for doing exactly that. The objection to the split infinitive is younger still, since it seems to have been almost unknown before the nineteenth century, when writers who admired Latin began to wish that English behaved more like it. Garrity presents these rules as if they were laws of nature, when they are no more laws of nature than the fashion for powdered wigs was.
>
> His treatment of the word "literally" is a case in point. Garrity calls its figurative use, as in "I literally died of embarrassment", a recent error that cannot be forgiven. Yet the evidence of any large corpus tells a different story, because Mark Twain wrote in 1876 that Tom Sawyer was "literally rolling in wealth", and examples of the same use can be found even earlier. Garrity's claim is not so much a cogent argument as an expression of taste, and he offers nothing to substantiate it.
>
> All the same, it would be unfair to suggest that the book has no value. When Garrity stops scolding and starts explaining, he can be genuinely useful. His chapter on syntax, in which he takes a single ninety-word sentence from an insurance company's letter and rebuilds it as four clear ones, is a small masterpiece.
>
> The deeper problem is that Garrity cannot tell the difference between language that is wrong and language that is merely unfamiliar to him. Colloquial English is not broken English, but English used for a different purpose, with grammatical rules that are every bit as strict as his, if a great deal less often written down. A teenager who says "she was like, no way" is not failing to use language properly, since the phrase reports an attitude rather than an exact utterance, which is something that "she said" cannot do.
>
> There is a hypothetical reader for whom this book would be ideal: someone anxious about a job application, who wants to know which choices might annoy the strictest possible reader, and who would rather not hear that many of the rules are mere conjecture. Everyone else should read the chapter on syntax twice and treat the rest as a fascinating record of one intelligent man's preferences, presented, quite sincerely, as the rules of the language. Mr. Garrity, I suspect, would object to a review that ends with a preposition, which is precisely the thing I have decided to finish with.

## Bài đọc 4 (Unit 1) — Bài báo khoa học phổ thông: vì sao ta hiếm khi nói thẳng

> **Why We Rarely Say What We Mean**
>
> Indirect speech looks wasteful, but one influential theory suggests that it is anything but.
>
> Imagine that you are at a dinner party and would like the salt. Almost nobody says "Give me the salt"; instead we say something like "If you could pass the salt, that would be great", an utterance which, taken verbatim, is an odd hypothetical statement. Nobody at the table is confused, of course, and nobody replies, "Yes, I suppose it would." Why, then, do we so often choose the long and uncertain road over the short and unequivocal one?
>
> The traditional answer is politeness: by alluding to a request rather than stating it, we leave the listener room to refuse, and so avoid sounding rude, as a direct order might. This explanation is sound as far as it goes, but it cannot account for the many cases in which the listener cannot really refuse.
>
> In 2008 the psychologist Steven Pinker and two colleagues posited a more interesting theory. Indirect speech, they argued, allows a speaker to communicate two things at once: a literal meaning that anyone could repeat without embarrassment, and an intended meaning that the listener understands perfectly well but that nobody could prove was there. In their best-known example, a driver stopped for speeding says to the officer something like, "I was wondering whether there might be some way to take care of this here, without all the paperwork." An honest officer can treat the remark as an innocent question about paying the fine, in which case the driver has lost nothing, while a dishonest one will understand it as an offer. The driver has gained what the authors call plausible deniability: the innuendo cannot be quoted back against him, because its semantic content is perfectly innocent.
>
> The theory becomes more cogent when it is applied to relationships rather than to crime. Drawing on the anthropologist Alan Fiske, the authors pointed out that people relate to one another under different tacit rules: friends share freely, colleagues exchange favors, and bosses give orders. A direct utterance forces both speakers to admit which set of rules they are using, whereas an indirect one leaves the question open. This is why a man who suggests to a colleague that they get a coffee sometime can step back without losing face if she replies, with admirable brevity, that she is rather busy this month. Neither of them has to admit that anything was proposed or refused, and the working relationship survives unchanged.
>
> There is something paradoxical in all this. We tend to think of clear speech as the mark of an honest and rational person, and of hints and allusions as the tools of those with something to hide. Yet the research suggests that speaking indirectly is often a form of care, a way of protecting the other person from the cost of a refusal that has been stated openly. A world without innuendo would not be more honest so much as more fragile, a place where every badly timed request ended in open rebuke or derision. So when a colleague calls your proposal "interesting", remember that the word was chosen precisely because it can mean almost anything, and that this lack of precision is, in its own way, a kindness.

## Bài đọc 5 (Unit 2) — Bản ghi podcast: cỗ máy cho bạn mọi trải nghiệm

> **The Machine That Gives You Everything**
>
> Transcript from the podcast Thinking Aloud, with host Fiona Walsh, philosopher Graham Price and psychologist Hannah Cole
>
> **Fiona:** Welcome back to Thinking Aloud. Today's question was first posed by the American philosopher Robert Nozick in 1974, and it remains one of the most argued-about thought experiments in philosophy. Suppose that scientists could connect you to a machine that would give you any experience you wished for, from the rapture of falling in love to the elation of writing a great novel, while your body floated quietly in a tank. You would believe that all of it was real, so would you plug in for life? Graham, you've taught this for twenty years.
>
> **Graham:** Most of my students say no, and they say it with surprising force. Nozick took that reaction as evidence against hedonism, the proposition that pleasure is the only thing of intrinsic value. If pleasure were all that mattered, the machine would be the rational choice, and yet almost nobody wants it. We seem to care about actually doing things, not merely having the experience of doing them, and about being in contact with a reality that we did not invent.
>
> **Fiona:** Hannah, you're a psychologist, so do you trust that reaction?
>
> **Hannah:** I trust that it's sincere, but I'm less sure that it tells us what Graham thinks it does. In 2010 the philosopher Felipe De Brigard turned the experiment around. He asked people to imagine that they had been in the machine all along, and then asked whether they would like to be disconnected and return to a real life that they knew nothing about. A large share of them preferred to stay exactly where they were. If authenticity were of paramount importance to us, the answer should have been the same in both directions, but it wasn't.
>
> **Graham:** That's a fair point, but it doesn't settle anything. People also refuse to leave bad jobs and unhappy homes, because fear of the unknown is no less real than the value of truth, and the two simply pull in opposite directions.
>
> **Hannah:** Exactly, and that's my worry: what looks like a deep axiom about reality might just be a preference for whatever we already have, which psychologists call status quo bias.
>
> **Fiona:** So is the original argument a fallacy?
>
> **Hannah:** Not a fallacy, perhaps, but a less convincing argument than it first appears.
>
> **Graham:** Let me put it another way, then. Imagine you discovered that your best friend had only pretended to love you for years, but had done it so well that you never suspected anything. Nothing in your experience would change, and yet almost everyone would say that something precious had been taken from them, whichever side of the machine they started from.
>
> **Hannah:** I'll give you the friend, because I would be indignant, and so would you. But I suspect that it's the friend I care about, not reality in some abstract sense.
>
> **Fiona:** We'll have to leave it there, in a state of uneasy equilibrium, which seems to be where philosophy is happiest.

## Bài đọc 6 (Unit 2) — Bài luận của Helena: "năng khiếu" và thói quen

> **The Knack and the Habit**
>
> Helena Brooks, for the Pine Street Library Newsletter, January
>
> After my first exhibition closed in September, a woman I had never met stopped me outside the library and told me, with great warmth, that I must have a natural knack for painting. I thanked her, because it was kindly meant, but the remark has stayed with me, slightly uncomfortable, like a coat that belongs to someone else. I have painted since I was a girl, and for most of those years almost everything I produced was, by any honest measure, mediocre.
>
> We like to believe that talent arrives as a gift, and there is a certain mystique around people who seem to do difficult things without effort. The idea flatters everyone, since the gifted feel chosen and the rest of us are excused from trying. Psychology has spent decades chipping away at this picture, partly by showing how firmly we hold on to it. In a study published in 2011, the psychologists Chia-Jung Tsay and Mahzarin Banaji asked professional musicians to listen to two pianists, one introduced as a "natural" who had shown remarkable ability very early, the other as a "striver" who had gotten there through hard work and perseverance. Asked directly, the musicians said that training mattered more than talent, yet they judged the natural to be more gifted and more likely to succeed, although both recordings were in fact the same pianist playing the same piece.
>
> Tsay later found the same "naturalness bias" among people judging entrepreneurs: to be rated as highly as a natural, a striver needed roughly four and a half more years of leadership experience, or around thirty more IQ points. Yet practice is not the whole story either, because when researchers combined the results of dozens of studies in 2014, they found that it explained only about an eighth of the differences in performance, rather more in games and music, and far less in professional work. The same research, however, leaves a great deal of room for something that is neither practice nor talent, which is the decision to keep going.
>
> I think of this as the habit rather than the knack. In my case it meant painting the same coffee cup, on the same windowsill, every Sunday morning for most of a year, while my daughter asked, with growing consternation, why I did not paint something interesting. Yet after about forty cups, I noticed that I had stopped drawing what I believed a cup should look like and had started drawing what was actually in front of me. That painting of a cold, slightly chipped cup was the one the newspaper critic singled out for praise, and nobody who saw it could have guessed how many dull mornings lay underneath it.
>
> I do not want to replace one myth with another. Tenacity is no more a guarantee of excellence than talent is, and there are tenacious people who practice diligently for decades without ever becoming more than competent. What I would like is a little less admiration for the knack and a little more respect for the habit, because the habit is the part that any of us can choose, by an act of volition, on an ordinary Sunday morning. My daughter, I should add, has now started painting her own cup, and she is already better at it than I was, which I find both delightful and slightly unfair.

## 💡 Mẹo làm bài

- Bài luận C2 hay nói **ngầm**: câu hỏi "imply / suggest" có đáp án KHÔNG nằm nguyên văn trong bài. Loại phương án nói quá (always, banned) trước.
- Ẩn dụ (cánh cửa khoá, con tàu) luôn được người viết **giải nghĩa ngay sau**. Đọc câu tiếp theo để chọn đáp án.
- `Be that as it may` = thừa nhận ý người khác nhưng vẫn giữ quan điểm mình. Câu hỏi "how does she respond" cần cả hai vế.
- `Were it not for X` = nếu không có X. Hỏi nghĩa câu thì đảo lại: vì có X nên mới có kết quả.
- Bài điểm sách C2 khen chê **có điều kiện** ("a fair description, provided that…"): phần điều kiện mới là ý thật của người viết.
- Bản ghi tranh luận: trước khi chọn đáp án, xác định **ai** nói câu đó và người kia **nhượng bộ** tới đâu ("I'll give you the friend, but…").
- `no more … than` = cả hai vế đều KHÔNG; `no less … than` = cả hai vế đều CÓ, mạnh ngang nhau.
