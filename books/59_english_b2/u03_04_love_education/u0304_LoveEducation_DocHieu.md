## Bài đọc 1 (Unit 3) — Tin nhắn đêm khuya giữa Leah và Chloe

> **Late-Night Messages: Leah and Chloe**
>
> Sunday, February 15, 1:12 a.m.
>
> **Leah:** Are you awake? I've really messed up.
>
> **Chloe:** I'm awake. What happened?
>
> **Leah:** Yesterday was Valentine's Day, and Ethan had booked a table at that little Italian place by the harbor. I got called in for an extra shift at four. I meant to text him, but the emergency room was crazy, and I only remembered at nine. He waited at the restaurant for an hour. When I finally called, he didn't shout. He just said, "It's fine," in that quiet voice, which is much worse.
>
> **Chloe:** Ugh. That's the voice of a man who is not fine.
>
> **Leah:** If only I had sent one message! It would have taken ten seconds. If I had sent that message, he wouldn't be upset now. The worst part is that it isn't the first time. With his shifts and mine, we see each other about twice a week, and sometimes I'm afraid that we're growing apart.
>
> **Chloe:** Can I be honest? You two are completely compatible. You're both kind, you both work too hard, and you both hate admitting that you're tired. The problem isn't love. It's the calendar.
>
> **Chloe:** When Dustin and I moved in together, we made a rule: no important conversations by text. Messages make everything sound colder than it really is.
>
> **Leah:** So what should I do?
>
> **Chloe:** Go and see him tomorrow. Say sorry first, and mean it. Then sit down together with both of your schedules and choose one evening a week that nobody can take away from you. Not the hospital, not the fire station.
>
> **Leah:** If only it were that easy.
>
> **Chloe:** It isn't easy. It's simple. Those are different things.
>
> **Leah:** You should write a column. OK. I'm going to bake him something terrible and go over there at ten.
>
> **Chloe:** Perfect. Nothing says "I'm sorry" like a burned cake. Now go to sleep!

## Bài đọc 2 (Unit 4) — Điểm số có giúp ta học tốt hơn?

> **Do Grades Help Us Learn?**
>
> For most students, a grade is the first thing they look at when an assignment comes back. Often, it is the only thing. That simple fact is at the center of one of the oldest debates in education: do grades help students learn, or do they get in the way?
>
> One of the best-known studies on the question was carried out in Israel in the late 1980s by the psychologist Ruth Butler. She gave school students a set of thinking tasks and then divided them into three groups. The first group received only written comments on their work, the second received only grades, and the third received both. When the students did similar tasks again, the comments-only group did the best, while the grades-only group did not improve at all. More surprisingly, the group that received grades and comments did no better than the grades-only group. According to Butler, once students saw a grade, they mostly ignored the comments.
>
> Critics of traditional grading point out that this matches what many teachers see every day: the more students focus on the number, the less attention they pay to how they could improve. Some also argue that grades push students toward easy choices: why take a difficult elective if it might lower your average?
>
> Others disagree. Grades, they argue, are quick, clear and easy to compare, which is why colleges and employers still rely on them. Many teachers also point out that writing detailed comments for 150 students takes far more time than giving a letter, and that some students are genuinely motivated by the chance to earn an A. And a single study, however famous, cannot prove that grades are harmful in every classroom.
>
> Perhaps the more useful of the two questions is not whether to grade, but when. Some teachers now give comments first and grades a few days later, so that students read the feedback before they see the number. Others let students rewrite an assignment after reading the comments. Whatever the approach, the goal is the same: the grade should be the end of an assignment, not the end of the learning.

## 💡 Mẹo làm bài

- `If only + had + V3` = tiếc nuối về quá khứ. `If only + quá khứ đơn / were` = ước điều khác ở hiện tại.
- Câu điều kiện hỗn hợp `If + had V3, … would + V … now`: việc quá khứ ảnh hưởng tới hiện tại. Chú ý từ `now`.
- Trong tin nhắn, giọng điệu quan trọng hơn chữ: "It's fine" nói bằng giọng lặng lẽ có thể nghĩa là không ổn.
- Bài hai mặt: gạch dưới các động từ dẫn ý kiến (`point out`, `argue`, `disagree`) để biết câu đó thuộc phía nào.
