## Bài đọc 1 (Unit 1) — Bản nháp bài luận xin vào đại học của Trevor

> **College Essay, Draft 2: More Than a Number on a Shirt**
>
> Written by: Trevor Carter
>
> For most of my life, people have identified me with one thing: soccer. I am the kid whose name is shouted from the stands, whose shirt has the number 9 on it, and whose grades are expected to be "fine for an athlete". For years, I accepted that label. It was easier than standing up for myself, and, to be honest, it felt good to be known for something.
>
> Last summer, an injury forced me to see myself from a different perspective. For three weeks, I could not run, and I came to the realization that I had no idea who I was without a ball at my feet. Earlier that year, I had joined my school's Green Club, originally because my best friend had joined it. Prior to that, I had never planted anything in my life. Collecting rainwater and sorting cafeteria trash do not sound exciting, I admit. But during those three weeks on the bench, the garden was the only place where I felt useful, and I discovered that I enjoy solving problems that nobody has asked me to solve.
>
> I am not claiming that I have outgrown soccer. I still train five days a week, and I still hate losing. However, I have grown into someone whose interests reach far beyond the field, and I would like to study environmental engineering, a subject that I would never have considered two years ago. I hope that you will see me as more than the number on my shirt; I am learning to see myself that way, too.
>
> Dad's notes in the margin:
>
> **Mitchell:** Much better than Draft 1. You've stopped listing trophies and started telling a story. Cut the line about grades being "fine for an athlete". It sounds bitter, and it may put readers off. But keep the part where you admit that you joined the club because of your friend. That kind of honesty is striking in an essay, and readers remember it. And I promise that I won't rewrite it for you. It has to sound like you, not like your history teacher.

## Bài đọc 2 (Unit 2) — Thứ tự sinh có quyết định tính cách?

> **Does Birth Order Shape Who We Are?**
>
> Ask a room full of people about birth order, and you will hear the same stories again and again. Firstborns are said to be responsible and serious, the leaders to whom younger siblings look for help. Youngest children are supposed to be charming and a little spoiled, and middle children, it is often claimed, are forgotten and spend their lives fighting for attention.
>
> The idea was made popular in the 1920s by the psychologist Alfred Adler, and it has been talked about ever since in books, magazines and family arguments. But when scientists finally tested it with large groups of people, the results were surprising.
>
> In 2015, researchers studied more than 20,000 adults in the United States, the United Kingdom and Germany. They compared people from different families, and also brothers and sisters from the same family. They found no meaningful link between birth order and personality: firstborns were not more responsible, and youngest children were not more charming. The only difference was a very small one in intelligence test scores, which were slightly higher for firstborns on average, but the gap was so tiny that you would not notice it inside a real family. Another study of over 370,000 American high school students came to almost the same conclusion.
>
> So why are so many parents sure that their children fit the stereotypes? One reason is that we usually compare siblings at the same moment, when they are different ages. Of course a twelve-year-old seems more responsible than her seven-year-old brother; she is five years older. We also tend to notice the examples that match what we already believe and forget the ones that don't.
>
> None of this means that family position is meaningless. An older child may be asked to look after younger ones, and that experience can matter. But researchers say that our upbringing, the age gap between siblings, the size of the family and the things that happen to us shape us far more than the simple fact of being born first, second or last.

## 💡 Mẹo làm bài

- `whose + danh từ` = của người đó. Một câu có thể lặp `whose` nhiều lần để liệt kê ("whose name…, whose shirt…").
- Động từ tường thuật mang thái độ: `admit` (thừa nhận điều khó nói), `claim` (khẳng định, chưa chắc đúng), `promise` (hứa).
- Câu hỏi chức năng ("Why does the writer mention…?"): xem câu ĐỨNG TRƯỚC ví dụ. Ví dụ thường minh hoạ cho câu đó.
- Bài phản bác một quan niệm thường có 3 phần: quan niệm → bằng chứng → giải thích vì sao người ta vẫn tin. Câu hỏi thái độ hay hỏi đoạn cuối, nơi người viết cân bằng lại.
