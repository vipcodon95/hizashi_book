## Phần 1 — Bài phát biểu của Tracy ở đám cưới Leah và Ethan

> **A Speech for Leah and Ethan**
>
> Given by Tracy Carter, maid of honor, at the wedding of Dr. Leah Carter and Ethan Hayes, Saturday, October 13, in the garden of Pine Street Library
>
> Good evening, everyone. When Leah asked me to give this speech, I asked her for advice, because that is what I have done about everything for as long as I can remember. She told me what she once told me before a job interview: answer the question, add a reason, give an example, and then stop. So I will try.
>
> The question is: why are these two right for each other? The reason is that each of them is brave in exactly the way that the other one is not. Ethan will run into a burning building without a second thought, but when he asked Leah to be his girlfriend, he was so nervous that he asked her to be his "emergency contact" instead. Leah, on the other hand, can tell a family the worst news of their lives with calm and kindness, but it took her three days and a long talk with Chloe to tell Ethan that she was hurt, the time he said yes to a training course before he had asked her.
>
> Now the example. Last Christmas morning, on the beach, Ethan knelt down, opened a small box, and dropped the ring in the sand. For ten minutes, the two of them searched on their hands and knees while a crowd of strangers joined in. It was Leah who found it. She held it up, and before Ethan could say a word, she said, "Yes. Obviously yes. Now please stand up." That, to me, is their whole marriage in one picture: he takes the risk, she finds what was lost, and somehow they end up laughing in front of strangers.
>
> In Ethan's family, I have learned, a child rings a small bell at the end of the toasts, and everyone has to stop talking and look at the person they came with. In my family, we have no such custom. We just cry. So, Amy, ring the bell. And now, as instructed, I will stop.

## Phần 2 — Bài khoa học: thói quen mất bao lâu để hình thành

> **How Long Does It Really Take to Form a Habit?**
>
> Ask almost anyone how long it takes to form a new habit, and you will probably hear the same answer: twenty-one days. The number appears in countless apps, articles and motivational videos, and it sounds wonderfully precise. It is, however, almost certainly wrong. The idea seems to have come from a popular book written in 1960 by a plastic surgeon, who noticed that his patients took about three weeks to get used to their new faces. He was describing a minimum, not a rule, and he was not studying habits at all.
>
> The best-known scientific attempt to answer the question was published in 2010 by a team at University College London. They asked 96 volunteers to choose one new daily behavior, such as eating a piece of fruit with lunch or running for fifteen minutes before dinner, and to report every day for twelve weeks how automatic it felt.
>
> For the typical volunteer, it took about 66 days for a behavior to reach its peak of automaticity, which is to say, the point at which it happened without much thought. However, that typical figure hides an enormous range. For some people, it took just 18 days; for others, the researchers estimated that it would take more than 250. Simple behaviors, like drinking a glass of water after breakfast, became automatic much faster than demanding ones, like doing fifty sit-ups.
>
> One finding is particularly encouraging. Missing a single day did not seriously damage the process. Insofar as there is a secret, it is not perfection but repetition in a consistent situation: the same action, at the same time or after the same cue, again and again.
>
> Why, then, does the myth survive? Partly because it is comforting. Three weeks sounds achievable; two months, or eight months, sounds daunting. But the comfort comes at a price. People who expect a habit to be easy after twenty-one days tend to conclude, on day twenty-two, that they have failed, and they give up just when the habit may be about to form. A more honest message would be less exciting but far more useful: it will probably take longer than you think, and that is perfectly normal.

>
> When I started my career, my first year was full of boring work: fixing small errors, writing tests, updating documents that nobody read. I complained about it constantly. I now realize that it was the most important training I ever received. Every small error I fixed taught me how the larger system worked, and every senior engineer who checked my work taught me what "good" looked like.
>
> Today, AI tools can do much of that boring work in seconds. For experienced engineers, this is a gift. For people trying to enter the profession, it may be a problem. A 2025 study by researchers at Stanford University, based on payroll records from millions of American workers, found that employment of workers aged 22 to 25 in the jobs most exposed to AI had fallen by more than a tenth compared with workers in less exposed jobs. Older workers in the same jobs were doing fine. The researchers were careful to say that other factors may have played a part, but the pattern is hard to ignore.
>
> Some managers see no problem here. If a machine can do a junior's work, they argue, why hire a junior? The flaw in this argument is obvious once you say it aloud: every senior engineer was once a junior. A company that stops training beginners today will have no experienced people in ten years' time.
>
> So what should we do? Not so much protect the old training as design a new one. On my team, every junior engineer now spends part of each week checking the AI's work, which turns out to be an excellent way to learn, because you cannot judge an answer that you do not understand. They also present their findings to a senior engineer, who asks them to explain each decision.
>
> A young engineer I know learned more from one mistake that she found and reported than from a whole year of easy tasks. Machines can do the easy tasks now. Our job is to make sure that beginners still get the chance to make, and learn from, the hard ones.

### Câu hỏi

**31. How does Nathan now see the boring work of his first year?**
*(Giờ Nathan nhìn những việc nhàm chán năm đầu thế nào?)*

- A. as a waste of time that taught him nothing *(như việc phí thời gian chẳng dạy anh điều gì)*
- B. as something only juniors should do, never seniors *(như việc chỉ người mới nên làm, người lâu năm thì không)*
- C. as the reason he nearly quit his first job *(như lý do anh suýt bỏ công việc đầu tiên)*
- D. as the most important training he ever received *(khoá đào tạo quan trọng nhất anh từng nhận)*

**32. What did fixing small errors teach Nathan?**
*(Việc sửa lỗi nhỏ dạy Nathan điều gì?)*

- A. how to work much faster than the others *(cách làm nhanh hơn hẳn những người khác)*
- B. how the larger system worked as a whole *(hệ thống lớn hơn vận hành thế nào như một tổng thể)*
- C. how to avoid senior engineers *(cách tránh kỹ sư lâu năm)*
- D. how to write documents that other people would read *(cách viết tài liệu mà người khác sẽ đọc)*

**33. Why might AI tools be "a gift" for some people and "a problem" for others?**
*(Vì sao công cụ AI có thể là "món quà" với người này và "vấn đề" với người khác?)*

- A. They are expensive for small companies, which must pay for each user. *(Chúng đắt với công ty nhỏ, vốn phải trả cho từng người dùng.)*
- B. Only experienced engineers can use them well enough, so beginners are left out of the profession. *(Chỉ kỹ sư giàu kinh nghiệm dùng đủ tốt, nên người mới bị loại khỏi nghề.)*
- C. They save experienced engineers time, but take over tasks beginners used to learn from. *(Chúng tiết kiệm thời gian cho kỹ sư giàu kinh nghiệm, nhưng lấy mất những việc người mới từng học từ đó.)*
- D. They make mistakes only when beginners use them, never experts. *(Chúng chỉ mắc lỗi khi người mới dùng, không bao giờ với chuyên gia.)*

**34. What did the 2025 Stanford study find?**
*(Nghiên cứu Stanford năm 2025 phát hiện điều gì?)*

- A. Employment of 22- to 25-year-olds in the jobs most exposed to AI fell by more than a tenth. *(Số người 22–25 tuổi có việc ở những công việc chịu tác động AI nhiều nhất giảm hơn một phần mười.)*
- B. All workers lost a tenth of their pay in the most exposed jobs. *(Mọi người lao động mất một phần mười lương ở những công việc chịu tác động nhiều nhất.)*
- C. Older workers lost their jobs faster than younger workers did. *(Người lớn tuổi mất việc nhanh hơn người trẻ.)*
- D. AI created a tenth more jobs for young workers than for older ones in total. *(AI tạo thêm một phần mười việc làm cho người trẻ so với người lớn tuổi.)*

**35. Why does Nathan mention that the researchers "were careful to say that other factors may have played a part"?**
*(Vì sao Nathan nhắc rằng các nhà nghiên cứu "thận trọng nói rằng có thể có những yếu tố khác góp phần"?)*

- A. to show that the study is worthless and should be ignored by everyone who reads it *(để cho thấy nghiên cứu vô giá trị và mọi người đọc nên bỏ qua nó)*
- B. to criticize Stanford for not checking its own numbers *(để chê Stanford không kiểm lại số liệu của mình)*
- C. to prove that AI is not the cause of the problem *(để chứng minh AI không phải nguyên nhân của vấn đề)*
- D. to present the evidence fairly, without claiming more than it shows *(để trình bày bằng chứng công bằng, không khẳng định quá những gì nó cho thấy)*

**36. What is the flaw in the argument "why hire a junior?"**
*(Lỗ hổng của lập luận "tuyển người mới làm gì?" là gì?)*

- A. Juniors are cheaper than machines, so companies should hire them. *(Người mới rẻ hơn máy nên công ty nên thuê họ.)*
- B. Machines cannot do any of the work that juniors do. *(Máy không làm được việc nào của người mới.)*
- C. Every senior was once a junior, so without beginners there will be no experts later. *(Người lâu năm nào cũng từng là người mới, nên không có người mới thì sau này sẽ không có chuyên gia.)*
- D. Managers do not understand how AI tools actually work. *(Quản lý không hiểu công cụ AI thực sự hoạt động ra sao.)*

**37. What does "Not so much protect the old training as design a new one" mean?**
*(Câu "Không phải bảo vệ cách đào tạo cũ mà là thiết kế một cách mới" nghĩa là gì?)*

- A. The goal is to create a new way of training rather than to keep the old one. *(Mục tiêu là tạo cách đào tạo mới chứ không giữ cách cũ.)*
- B. The old training should be protected at all costs, whatever the price and effort. *(Phải bảo vệ cách đào tạo cũ bằng mọi giá, dù tốn kém và công sức đến đâu.)*

- D. Machines should design the training for the new engineers. *(Máy nên thiết kế việc đào tạo cho kỹ sư mới.)*

**38. Why is checking the AI's work a good way for juniors to learn?**

## 💡 Mẹo làm bài

- **Quản lý thời gian:** 60 phút cho 4 bài. Phần 1 khoảng 13 phút, Phần 2 khoảng 15 phút, Phần 3 khoảng 14 phút, Phần 4 khoảng 18 phút.
- **Bài phát biểu:** người nói thường báo trước cấu trúc (`answer the question, add a reason, give an example`) rồi làm đúng như thế. Câu cuối hay khép vòng với câu đầu.
- **Bài khoa học:** phân biệt **con số điển hình** với **khoảng dao động** (`that typical figure hides an enormous range`). Câu hỏi hay bẫy bằng phương án "áp dụng cho mọi người".
- **Báo cáo:** so con số thật với mục tiêu (`a little below the four hours`). Cụm nói giảm (`to put it politely`) là chỗ hỏi hàm ý.
- **Bài quan điểm C1:** người viết nêu cả **giới hạn của bằng chứng** (`other factors may have played a part`). Câu hỏi thái độ: họ trình bày công bằng, không phải bác bỏ nghiên cứu.
