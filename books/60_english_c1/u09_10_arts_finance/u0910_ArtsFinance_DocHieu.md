## Bài đọc 1 (Unit 9) — Bài phê bình triển lãm tranh đầu tiên của Helena

> **Review: "Bridges and Small Things" by Helena Brooks**
>
> By Jordan Wells, Weekend Arts page. At the gallery of Pine Street Library until September 30. Free entry.
>
> Helena Brooks is not, by her own admission, a professional artist. She is a civil engineer who has spent more than fifteen years designing bridges and painting on weekends, and this exhibition of twenty-four works is her first. I went expecting the kind of pleasant, slightly amateur show that libraries often host. What I found was more interesting, and more uneven, than that. It is, in other words, a debut, and it deserves to be judged as one: not by asking whether it is perfect, but by asking whether it shows a voice of its own.
>
> The small paintings are the heart of the show. A pair of child's sneakers left on a back step. A cup of coffee going cold on a windowsill. The silhouette of an old man feeding birds in a park at dusk. So quiet are these pictures that you could easily walk past them, and yet each one rewards a second look. The light is handled with an engineer's precision, and the mood is reminiscent of Edward Hopper's empty streets, though warmer and less lonely.
>
> Beside each painting hangs a short poem by the artist. This I did not expect, and I was ready to dislike it. In fact, the poems are often wry and unconventional, and they give the show a gentle sense of humor. Under the cold coffee, for example, she has written: "Someone was called away. / The coffee waited. / It always does."
>
> As for the large panoramic paintings of bridges that give the show half of its title, they are, I am sorry to say, the weakest work here. They are technically impressive, every cable and beam in its place, but they feel like drawings for a client rather than paintings for a viewer. One of them, a huge sunset over the new bridge at Valley Springs, is frankly showy, with colors that need toning down. Next to the small works, most of them seem lackluster, and the open sketchbooks displayed beside them, full of quick pencil studies of the same bridges, are far more alive. It is as if the engineer and the painter had not quite agreed who was in charge.
>
> Is this, then, a show worth visiting? Without question. Uneven first exhibitions are often more alive than polished later ones, and the small works here evoke something that much bigger art often misses: the feeling that ordinary moments are worth noticing. My advice to the artist would be to trust the small things. My advice to readers is simpler: go, and take your time. And isn't that, in the end, what these paintings are asking of us?

### Câu hỏi

**1. What did the critic expect before visiting the exhibition?**
*(Trước khi tới xem, nhà phê bình đã đoán điều gì?)*

- A. a famous artist's latest work in a big city museum *(tác phẩm mới nhất của một hoạ sĩ nổi tiếng trong một bảo tàng lớn)*
- B. an exhibition about engineering and bridge design *(một triển lãm về kỹ thuật và thiết kế cầu)*
- C. a pleasant but slightly amateur show *(một triển lãm dễ chịu nhưng hơi nghiệp dư)*
- D. a show that would be closed on the day he visited *(một triển lãm sẽ đóng cửa vào ngày ông đến)*

**2. What does the word "uneven" suggest about the exhibition?**
*(Từ "uneven" gợi ý gì về triển lãm?)*

- A. Some works are much stronger than others. *(Có tác phẩm mạnh hơn hẳn những tác phẩm khác.)*
- B. The paintings are hung at different heights around the gallery. *(Tranh được treo ở những độ cao khác nhau quanh phòng tranh.)*
- C. The gallery floor is not flat, so visitors must walk carefully. *(Sàn phòng tranh không phẳng nên khách phải đi cẩn thận.)*
- D. The show is too short for the number of works it contains. *(Triển lãm quá ngắn so với số tác phẩm trưng bày.)*

**3. What do the small paintings mainly show?**
*(Các bức tranh nhỏ chủ yếu vẽ gì?)*

- A. famous bridges seen from far away at sunset *(những cây cầu nổi tiếng nhìn từ xa lúc hoàng hôn)*
- B. ordinary everyday moments and objects *(những khoảnh khắc và đồ vật bình thường hằng ngày)*
- C. abstract shapes in bright colors without any objects *(hình khối trừu tượng nhiều màu rực rỡ, không có đồ vật nào)*
- D. portraits of famous people and their families *(chân dung người nổi tiếng và gia đình họ)*

**4. What does the sentence beginning "So quiet are these pictures" suggest?**
*(Câu bắt đầu bằng "So quiet are these pictures" gợi ý điều gì?)*

- A. The paintings are too dark to see unless visitors use a light. *(Tranh quá tối, khách phải dùng đèn mới nhìn được.)*
- B. Visitors are asked to be quiet in front of the paintings. *(Khách được yêu cầu giữ im lặng trước các bức tranh.)*
- C. The paintings should be bigger so that visitors will not miss them. *(Tranh nên to hơn để khách khỏi bỏ sót.)*
- D. The paintings are modest and easy to miss, but they repay careful attention. *(Tranh khiêm tốn, dễ bị bỏ qua, nhưng đáng được nhìn kỹ.)*

**5. How did the critic's opinion of the poems change?**
*(Ý kiến của nhà phê bình về các bài thơ thay đổi thế nào?)*

- A. He liked them at first, but later found them boring and too long. *(Ban đầu ông thích, nhưng sau thấy chúng chán và quá dài.)*
- B. He did not read them because he expected to dislike them. *(Ông không đọc vì nghĩ mình sẽ không thích.)*
- C. He was ready to dislike them, but found them witty and original. *(Ông đã sẵn sàng để không thích, nhưng lại thấy chúng hóm hỉnh và độc đáo.)*
- D. He thought they should be removed because they distracted from the paintings. *(Ông nghĩ nên gỡ chúng xuống vì làm xao nhãng khỏi tranh.)*

*(Nhà phê bình muốn nói gì qua câu "người kỹ sư và người hoạ sĩ chưa thống nhất được ai là người cầm trịch"?)*

- A. Two different people painted the pictures and they could not agree on the style or the colors. *(Hai người khác nhau vẽ tranh và họ không thống nhất được về phong cách hay màu sắc.)*

- C. Helena argued with the library about who should choose the paintings. *(Helena cãi nhau với thư viện về việc ai chọn tranh.)*
- D. The bridge was built badly because the engineer and the painter disagreed. *(Cây cầu xây kém vì người kỹ sư và người hoạ sĩ bất đồng.)*

**9. Overall, what is the critic's verdict?**
*(Nhìn chung, kết luận của nhà phê bình là gì?)*

- A. The show is uneven but clearly worth visiting, mainly for the small works. *(Triển lãm không đều tay nhưng rõ ràng đáng xem, chủ yếu nhờ các tác phẩm nhỏ.)*

- C. Only the bridge paintings are worth seeing, because they are the most skillful. *(Chỉ tranh cầu là đáng xem vì chúng điêu luyện nhất.)*
- D. Helena should stop painting and return to her work as an engineer. *(Helena nên thôi vẽ và quay lại làm kỹ sư.)*

**10. What is the function of the final question, "isn't that, in the end, what these paintings are asking of us?"**
*(Câu hỏi cuối "chẳng phải đó chính là điều những bức tranh này đang đòi hỏi ở chúng ta sao?" có chức năng gì?)*

- A. to ask readers to write to the newspaper with their opinions of the show *(để mời bạn đọc viết thư cho báo nêu ý kiến về triển lãm)*
- B. to show that the critic is unsure whether the show is worth visiting *(để cho thấy nhà phê bình không chắc triển lãm có đáng xem không)*
- C. to link his advice to take your time with the paintings' message about ordinary moments *(để nối lời khuyên đừng vội với thông điệp của tranh về những khoảnh khắc bình thường)*
- D. to criticize the library for charging nothing for such a good show *(để chê thư viện vì không thu tiền một triển lãm hay như vậy)*

## Bài đọc 2 (Unit 10) — Bản ghi nhớ của Nathan: ba phương án cho tiệm bên cạnh

> **Memo: What Should We Do With the Shop Next Door?**
>
> To: Harriet Brooks. From: Nathan. Date: August 26
>
> Mom, you asked me to put the numbers on paper before you decide about the old bakery next door, which has now been empty for more than a year. Here they are. I have tried to be honest rather than encouraging.
>
> Option A: expand the café into the shop. This would double the number of seats. The rent is $3,200 a month, and turning a bakery into a café would cost around $45,000, most of which we would have to borrow. If the extra seats were full at lunchtime, the loan would be paid off in about three years. But more seats do not automatically mean more customers, and in an economic downturn, a bigger café would lose money faster than a small one would. We would also need two more staff, which would increase our monthly expenditure substantially, and a second coffee machine alone would mean forking out another $9,000. The bakery closed for exactly that reason, as did the bookstore on the corner before it.
>
> Option B: open a shared kitchen. The bakery already has two large ovens and a professional kitchen, so the refurbishment would cost far less, about $12,000. Home cooks and small food businesses, such as the families who cooked for The Neighbors' Table two autumns ago, could rent the kitchen by the hour. Several have already asked whether such a place exists. The arrangement would also be mutually useful: the cooks would get affordable space, and the café would gain prospective customers every time someone came to collect their food. ## Bài đọc 3 (Unit 9) — Bài phê bình phim: phần tiếp theo của một bom tấn mùa hè

> **Review: "After the Last Ferry"**
>
> By Wesley Pratt, Film Desk. In theaters from Friday.
>
> Sequels to summer blockbusters rarely deserve the word "surprise," and I went into After the Last Ferry expecting very little. Three years ago, The Last Ferry, the debut of the screenwriter and director Nora Quinn, became the most riveting thriller of the summer, the story of a ferry captain who must get forty passengers across a stormy bay before the old bridge behind them falls. Its success made a sequel almost certain, and sequels that are made because the first film earned a fortune tend to be louder, longer and emptier than the films they follow.
>
> This one is not. Rather than repeating the storm, Quinn's new screenplay begins a year later, when the protagonist, Captain Hazel Alder, is no longer a hero to anyone, least of all to herself. The town has built a new bridge, the ferry has been sold, and Hazel spends her days repairing other people's boats and refusing to talk about that night. The heroine of the first film, in other words, has become a woman trying very hard to be forgotten.
>
> Much of the story is told in flashback, a device that usually slows a film down. Here it does the opposite. Each return to the night of the storm reveals something the first film left out: a passenger Hazel could not reach, a decision she made in a few seconds and has questioned every day since. So carefully are these scenes placed that the past seems to be catching up with the present, until the two finally meet in the climax.
>
> As for the performances, they are strong across the cast. Joan Linden, returning as Hazel, gives a portrayal of guilt that is quiet, precise and never sentimental; she does not ask us to pity her, which is exactly why we do. Her best scene contains almost no dialogue: Hazel, alone in her kitchen, sings an old sea song under her breath, and her voice breaks on the one line she cannot finish.
>
> The film is not without faults. Its climactic scene, a rescue in a second storm, is staged with all the noise and money of the first film, and it feels borrowed from a different, more ordinary movie. After two hours of careful, human drama, I wanted the director to trust her story and tone down the thunder. A second story about an insurance investigator is also never finished, as if a whole section of the screenplay had been cut in the editing room.
>
> These are real weaknesses, but small ones. What stays with you is the film's honest depiction of what happens to people after the cameras have left: the hero who is not sure she was one, the town that would rather celebrate than remember. How many sequels can say they understood their original better than it understood itself? Not many. This much I can promise: go and see it, ideally in a quiet theater, and stay until the final shot.

## Bài đọc 4 (Unit 9) — Bài khoa học phổ thông: vì sao có phòng hoà nhạc nghe hay hơn

> **Why Some Concert Halls Sound Better Than Others**
>
> By Celia Marsh, science writer
>
> Imagine hearing the same piano concerto, played by the same orchestra, in two different halls. In the first, the music seems to surround you, warm and full, as if the walls themselves were playing. In the second, it sounds thin and distant, and the soft passages almost disappear. Nothing has changed except the room, and yet the experience could hardly be more different.
>
> For most of history, builders achieved good acoustics largely by luck, or by copying halls that already worked. That changed in 1895, when a young Harvard physics teacher named Wallace Sabine was asked to fix a new lecture hall in which students could barely understand the speaker. Working at night, when the building was quiet, Sabine carried hundreds of seat cushions in and out of the room and measured how long a sound took to fade away. What he discovered was surprisingly simple: the time a sound continues depends on the size of a room and on how much of the sound its surfaces absorb.
>
> This continuing sound, called reverberation, is the key to how a hall sounds. Too little, and music seems dry and lifeless; too much, and each note runs into the next, so that speech in particular becomes a blur. For a lecture, about one second is ideal, whereas an orchestra generally sounds best with nearer two. Sabine's work so impressed the city's orchestra that he was invited to help design Boston's Symphony Hall, which opened in 1900 and is still praised as one of the best in the world.
>
> Reverberation, however, is not the whole story. Studies in recent decades suggest that listeners care just as much about where the sound comes from. In a tall, narrow hall shaped like a shoebox, much of the sound reaches each listener after bouncing off the side walls, and these reflections from the left and right seem to make the music feel richer and more involving. This may explain why several of the most admired halls, some of them built long before anyone understood why they worked, share this plain, rectangular shape.
>
> Modern architects, perhaps not surprisingly, are reluctant to build only boxes. Many prefer more adventurous designs, with seats rising in steps all around the stage, which bring the audience nearer to the musicians and are often more aesthetically pleasing. Such halls can sound wonderful, but they demand far more careful engineering, since the side walls that do so much work in a shoebox are largely missing. Today's designers test their ideas with computer models long before building begins, and many add panels and curtains that can be moved so that the same room can suit a solo acoustic guitar one evening and a full choir singing in unison the next.
>
> None of this means that great acoustics can be calculated like a bridge. Even with all our modern tools, the final test is still a human one: an audience sitting in the dark, listening, and deciding whether the room has disappeared.

## Bài đọc 5 (Unit 10) — Tóm tắt nghiên cứu: vì sao tiền bất ngờ dễ bị tiêu hoang

> **Why Unexpected Money Feels Like Free Money**
>
> A research summary for the Money section
>
> Suppose you find a hundred-dollar bill in the pocket of an old coat. Would you spend it more freely than a hundred dollars from your salary? Most people admit that they would, and that small confession sits at the heart of one of the most influential ideas in modern economics.
>
> In the 1980s, the economist Richard Thaler, who would later receive the Nobel Prize, began describing what he called mental accounting. His point was not so much that people are bad with money as that they divide it into separate "accounts" in their heads, each with its own rules. Money for rent is treated seriously, money for a holiday can be spent without guilt, and money that arrives unexpectedly, such as a bonus, a gift or a small inheritance, often feels as if it does not quite count.
>
> In strict economic terms, this makes little sense. A dollar is a dollar wherever it comes from, and a family that splashes out on a new television with a surprise refund is exactly as rich, or as poor, as a family that buys the same television with its salary. Yet the habit is remarkably hard to break, and it shapes behavior in more ways than is generally believed.
>
> Consider investors. Researchers have found that people who have just made a profit are often more willing to take risks with it, as if the gains were somehow not really theirs. Or consider the many households that keep savings in an account paying almost no interest while paying high interest on a credit card or an overdraft at the same time. Paying off the debt would leave them better off, but emptying the "savings" account feels like a sacrifice, even though the debt costs them far more than the savings earn.
>
> Mental accounting is not entirely foolish, however. Many people use it deliberately as a form of self-control: by labeling one account "emergency fund" and refusing to touch it, they build a buffer against sudden changes that willpower alone might never have produced. Similarly, someone who receives an inheritance from a grandparent may choose to keep it apart, not for financial reasons but because it carries sentimental value. The rules, in other words, can protect us as well as mislead us.
>
> What Thaler's work suggests, then, is not that we should get rid of our mental accounts but that we should notice them. Before spending money you did not expect, it is worth asking a simple question: if this had come from my salary, would I still spend it this way? If the answer is no, the money is probably being squandered, not because it was free, but because we treated it as if it were.

## Bài đọc 6 (Unit 10) — Blog của Chloe: bốn tháng mở studio và bài học về dòng tiền

> **Four Months In: What Nobody Told Me About Cash Flow**
>
> Chloe Bennett, on the Bennett Studio blog
>
> When I opened Bennett Studio in May, I thought the hardest part would be finding clients. I was wrong. We found clients faster than expected; what nearly sank us in August was getting paid.
>
> Here is how it happened. In June we finished three large projects, including a new logo and menu for a restaurant on Ocean Avenue, and I sent out invoices worth just over $11,000. On paper, it was our best month ever. In practice, the money arrived in pieces: one invoice was paid after forty days, another after sixty, and the third only after I had sent four increasingly polite emails. Meanwhile, the rent, Ruby's and Sam's pay and the software we depend on all had to be paid on the first of every month, whether our clients had paid us or not.
>
> By the middle of August, I had racked up a bank overdraft that I was ashamed to tell anyone about, Ruby included. Although the studio was profitable on paper, we were, for about three weeks, almost out of cash. That is the difference between profit and cash flow, and nobody at art school ever explained it to me.
>
> My best friend Leah, who is far better with spreadsheets than I am, sat with me one evening and helped me make four changes. I share them here because I suspect I am not the only freelancer who has learned this the hard way.
>
> 1. We now ask for half of the fee before we start any project worth more than $1,000; prospective clients who refuse to pay anything in advance are, in my estimation, telling us something useful.
>
> 2. Every invoice is issued on the day the work is delivered, not when I find time, and payment is due in fourteen days rather than thirty.
>
> 3. We set aside a fixed fraction of every payment, currently fifteen percent, in a separate account that nobody is allowed to touch. It is our buffer for the slack season, which for designers usually arrives in January, when clients have spent their budgets.
>
> 4. I stopped saying yes to every job, because a small project from a difficult client can cost more in time and stress than it earns, however good it looks in our portfolio.
>

## 💡 Mẹo làm bài

- Bài phê bình C1 hiếm khi chỉ khen hoặc chỉ chê. Tìm cặp **mạnh nhất / yếu nhất** (`the heart of the show` ↔ `the weakest work here`) để trả lời câu hỏi kết luận.
- Đảo ngữ `So quiet are these pictures that…` nhấn mạnh tính chất; nghĩa thật thường nằm ở vế sau (`and yet each one rewards a second look`).
- Câu hỏi tu từ cuối bài (`isn't that…?`) **không hỏi**, mà khẳng định thông điệp chính.
- Bản ghi nhớ so sánh phương án: chú ý cặp `at its best` / `at its worst`, và câu `not so much A as B` cho biết người viết coi B mới là câu hỏi thật.
- Bài phê bình phim: câu hỏi tu từ ở cuối (`How many sequels can say…?`) thường được tác giả **tự trả lời** ngay (`Not many.`) — đó là lời khen mạnh, không phải câu hỏi thật.
- Bài khoa học phổ thông: đoạn mở bằng **tình huống tưởng tượng** (`Imagine…`) để dẫn vào vấn đề; câu kết của đoạn (`Nothing has changed except the room`) mới là ý chính.
- Tóm tắt nghiên cứu: tìm từ chuyển ý `however`, `similarly`, `in other words` để thấy tác giả đang **cân lại** lập luận (mặt hại → mặt lợi).
- Blog trải nghiệm: cặp `on paper` / `in practice` báo hiệu khoảng cách giữa sổ sách và thực tế — câu hỏi suy luận hay rơi vào chỗ này.
