## Bài đọc 1 (Unit 7) — Mitchell kể chuyện làm bồi thẩm đoàn

> **Twelve Strangers and One Question**
>
> Mitchell Carter, for the Westview High staff blog
>
> For twenty years, I have taught teenagers how trials work. Last month, for the first time in my life, I was chosen to sit on a jury, and I discovered how little I really knew.
>
> The case seemed simple. A young man was accused of stealing tools worth several thousand dollars from a building site. The proceedings lasted four days. The prosecution had a blurry security video of a man in a gray hoodie, a witness who had seen a white van that night, and the fact that the young man owned a gray hoodie and had once worked at the site. He did not own a white van, and none of the tools were ever found.
>
> Before we began, the judge gave us two instructions that I have been thinking about ever since. First, we were not to read anything about the case or search online, regardless of how curious we were. Second, we could find him guilty only if the evidence proved it beyond a reasonable doubt, and our decision had to be unanimous: all twelve of us had to agree.
>
> When we first went around the table in the jury room, nine of us said guilty and three said not guilty. I was one of the nine. Notwithstanding the weak evidence, I had made up my mind by the end of the first day, and I am not proud of the reason. He looked nervous, he avoided eye contact, and some part of me had decided that an innocent man would look calmer.
>
> It was a retired nurse named Mrs. Ellison who changed my mind. She asked us, very politely, to look at the video again, and to say honestly whether we could see a face. We could not. Then she asked how many people in San Diego own a gray hoodie. Several of us were wearing one. For all our certainty, we had no real grounds for it.
>
> After two days of discussion, we returned a verdict of not guilty. I want to be precise about what that means. It does not mean that we were sure he was innocent; it means that the evidence was not strong enough to prove that he was guilty. Those are two very different things, and the difference is the whole point of the system.
>
> Should you ever be called for jury duty, please do not try to avoid it. Go, listen, and watch your own first impressions very carefully, as Mrs. Ellison taught me to. It was the most important lesson I have learned in a long time, albeit not one that I can easily teach in a classroom.

## Bài đọc 2 (Unit 8) — Bài khoa học: AlphaFold và câu đố năm mươi năm

> **The Fifty-Year Puzzle**
>
> Almost everything that happens in your body depends on proteins. They carry oxygen in your blood, fight bacteria, and allow your muscles to move. Each protein is a long chain of smaller molecules, and in order to work, that chain must fold itself into a very particular three-dimensional shape. Its function hinges on that shape, so much so that a single mutation which changes the shape can cause a serious disease.
>
> For about fifty years, predicting that shape from the chain alone was one of the great unsolved problems in biology. In 1972, the chemist Christian Anfinsen received a Nobel Prize partly for showing that the chain contains all the information needed to find the shape. In principle, then, it should be possible to calculate. In practice, the number of possible shapes is so huge that testing them one by one would take longer than the age of the universe.
>
> Scientists therefore had to measure each shape in the laboratory, using methods that required great precision and, often, years of work for a single protein. Since 1994, research teams have taken part in a competition every two years in which they try to predict shapes that have been measured but not yet published.
>
> For decades, progress was slow. Then, in 2020, an AI system called AlphaFold, developed by the company DeepMind, produced predictions that were, for many proteins, about as accurate as the laboratory measurements themselves. The momentum since then has been remarkable. A free public database launched in 2021 with just over 360,000 predictions, and it subsequently grew to around 200 million, covering almost every protein known to science. Researchers around the world have since used the predictions in projects ranging from new medicines to enzymes that can break down plastic waste. In 2024, the Nobel Prize in Chemistry was shared between two of AlphaFold's creators, Demis Hassabis and John Jumper, and the scientist David Baker, a pioneer in designing completely new proteins.
>
> It would be easy to conclude that the puzzle is solved and that laboratories are no longer needed. That would be a mistake. A prediction is still an approximation, and its reliability varies from one protein to another. Proteins also move, change shape and work together, and the extent to which AI can predict that behavior is still limited. Researchers still need experiments to check whether a prediction is right.
>
> What has changed is where they start. Instead of spending years finding a shape, a scientist can now begin with a good guess in minutes, and spend those years asking what the shape means. Insofar as science is a search for better questions, that may be the most important change of all.

## 💡 Mẹo làm bài

- Câu hỏi NOT/EXCEPT về bằng chứng: đọc cả câu **ngay sau** danh sách. `none of the tools were ever found` loại luôn phương án "tìm thấy dụng cụ".
- Người kể tự nhận lỗi (`I am not proud of the reason`) là chỗ hỏi thái độ và bài học. Lý do nằm ở câu kế tiếp.
- `In principle … In practice …` là cặp đối lập kinh điển. Câu hỏi "why difficult in practice" lấy vế sau.
- Bài khoa học C1 thường có đoạn **chặn kết luận vội** (`It would be easy to conclude… That would be a mistake.`). Phương án tuyệt đối (always wrong, no longer needed) gần như luôn sai.
