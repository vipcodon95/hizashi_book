## Bài đọc 1 (Unit 1) — Báo cáo của Megan về dự án đổi cách giao ca

> **Changing How We Hand Over: A Report After Twelve Weeks**
>
> From: Megan Carter, charge nurse, Emergency Department, Sunset Bay Hospital. To: all department staff and the department director.
>
> Last winter was the hardest that most of us can remember. With the workload rising week after week, three experienced nurses left the department within two months, and the ones who stayed were running on fatigue and coffee. When we asked staff where things most often went wrong, the same answer kept coming back: the handover between shifts, at seven in the morning and seven at night, when information about forty patients is passed on in a crowded corridor in a hurry. It was high time we changed it.
>
> In September, we initiated a twelve-week trial of a new protocol. Handover now takes place at the bedside, so that the patient can correct us if we get something wrong, and it follows a printed checklist of five points. The biggest constraint was time. The director agreed to allocate a fifteen-minute overlap between shifts, paid as overtime, on the condition that we measured whether it was worth the money.
>
> So, was it? In the twelve weeks before the trial, staff reported fourteen cases of important information being missed at handover. During the trial, they reported five. I should be honest about the costs, too. Handover takes longer than before, about thirteen minutes per nurse instead of nine, and several colleagues have told me that the checklist feels tedious when a patient's situation is simple.
>
> One part of the trial clearly did not work: the night shift. Patients who had finally fallen asleep were woken up by four nurses talking beside their bed, which helped nobody. For the night handover, we are set to try a quieter version at the door of each room, using a tablet instead of paper.
>
> My recommendation is that we keep the new protocol for day handovers and review it again in March. The director is due to make a final decision in January.
>
> A personal note to finish. I have been a nurse in this department for more than twenty years, and last winter I was on the verge of leaving myself. What kept me here was not the pay but the people, and this project has reminded me that retention is not only about money. It is about whether we believe that our ideas can change the place where we work. I would like to commend every nurse who tried something new while already exhausted. You did the hard part.

## Bài đọc 2 (Unit 2) — Leah viết về hiệu ứng người ngoài cuộc

> **Will Anyone Help? What the Cameras Really Show**
>
> By Dr. Leah Carter, for the Sunset Bay Hospital staff newsletter
>
> Most of us learned about the "bystander effect" in a first-year psychology class: the more people who see an emergency, the less likely it is that any one of them will help. The idea seems plausible. Each person presumes that somebody else has already called for help, and nobody wants to be the one who makes a fuss about nothing.
>
> Laboratory experiments in the late 1960s seemed to support it. Students who believed they were the only witness to a medical emergency were far more likely to act than students who believed that others were listening too.
>
> However, a laboratory is not a street. In 2020, a team of researchers published a study that did something nobody had done on this scale before: they watched what really happens. They studied 219 arguments and fights in public places, recorded by security cameras in three countries, namely the United Kingdom, the Netherlands and South Africa.
>
> The result was startling. In about nine out of ten cases, at least one bystander, and usually several, stepped in to help, by calming someone down, pulling someone away or standing between two people. Even more surprising, the more people there were, the more likely it was that somebody would help. The very thing that was supposed to stop people from acting turned out to make help more likely.
>
> Does this mean that the old idea was simply wrong? Not quite. Each individual in a large crowd may still be a little less likely to act; it is just that a large crowd contains more people who might. Hesitation is real, too. People will often stand and watch for a few seconds while they look for cues from those around them, and in a medical emergency, those seconds matter.
>
> This is where my partner Ethan, who teaches first-aid classes for the fire department, has a simple rule. Never shout "Somebody call 911!" at a crowd. Point at one person and say, "You, in the red jacket, call 911 and tell me when they answer." An explicit request removes the doubt. The person in the red jacket no longer has to guess whether someone else has done it.
>
> I find the 2020 study reassuring, and I think it should make us less cynical about strangers. Most people are not indifferent; they are simply waiting for someone to tell them what to do. In our job, that someone is very often us.

## 💡 Mẹo làm bài

- **Báo cáo:** câu hỏi mục đích lấy ở phần đề xuất (`My recommendation is…`). Câu hỏi chức năng đoạn: xem đoạn đó trả lời câu hỏi nào (`So, was it?`).
- `be set to` / `be due to` = **chưa xảy ra**. Câu hỏi suy luận hay bẫy bằng phương án "đã thử và hiệu quả".
- Bài khoa học C1 thường **không bác bỏ hẳn** ý cũ: `Not quite`, `it is just that…` nghĩa là ý cũ đúng một phần. Tránh phương án tuyệt đối (completely true / completely false).
- Câu hỏi quy chiếu (`The very thing`, `that someone`) luôn trả lời bằng câu ngay trước nó.
