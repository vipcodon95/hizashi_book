## Bài đọc 1 (Unit 11) — Blog Nathan: khác biệt giao tiếp trong một nhóm ba thành phố

> **What I Got Wrong About Silence**
>
> Nathan Brooks, team lead, on his personal blog
>
> A year ago, I became the lead of a team of nine software engineers in three cities: San Diego, Chicago and Lisbon. I had read the articles about working across cultures, and I thought I was prepared. I was not, and the reason surprised me. Our first meeting in person, in Lisbon, went wonderfully: we mingled, ate far too much, and laughed at one another's accents. It was afterwards, on video calls, that the trouble began.
>
> In our weekly meeting, I would propose an idea and ask, "Any objections?" Usually there was silence, and I took silence to mean that everyone concurred. Then, three weeks into a project, an engineer in Chicago, whom I will call Greg, sent me a long, careful message explaining why he had thought from the very first day that my plan would not work. He was right. We had lost three weeks.
>
> When I asked him why he had said nothing in the meeting, his answer was candid. Much as he respected me, he did not feel it was his place to question the team lead in front of eight other people. In the team he had come from, he said, you raised doubts privately, with tact, and only after you had checked your facts. It had never occurred to him that I might read his silence as agreement, any more than it had occurred to me that he might read my question as a test.
>
> Here is the part that the articles had not prepared me for. The most direct person on the team, who will happily blurt out "That won't work" before I have finished a sentence, is Tomás, in Lisbon. The quietest is Greg, who grew up twenty minutes from my house in San Diego. Whatever the articles say about this country or that one, the way a person disagrees seems to depend at least as much on their family, their personality and the teams they have worked in as on where they were born.
>
> That does not mean that culture is irrelevant. It means that it is by no means a reliable shortcut. Treating Tomás as "the Portuguese one" and Greg as "the American one" would have told me nothing useful about either of them.
>
> So we changed how we work. At the start of each project, every member now answers three questions: How do you prefer to disagree? How quickly do you expect a reply? What does "urgent" mean to you? We also stopped asking "Any objections?" in meetings. Instead, I ask each person, by name, for one risk they can see in the plan, which makes raising a doubt part of the job rather than a challenge to the boss.
>
> It is far from perfect, and it does feel a little awkward at first. But try as I might, I cannot remember a single silence in our meetings since then that turned out to mean yes.

## Bài đọc 2 (Unit 12) — Bài quan điểm của Leah về AI ghi chép trong phòng khám

> **The Third Listener in the Room**
>
> Dr. Leah Carter, Emergency Department, for the Sunset Bay Hospital staff newsletter
>
> For the past three months, I have been one of twenty doctors testing an AI note-taker. With the patient's permission, a small app on my phone listens to our conversation and, by the time I leave the room, has written a draft of my notes. Over the past few years, many hospitals have begun to try tools like this, and I want to weigh in on the debate before we decide whether to use it in every department.
>
> The benefits are real. Before the trial, I spent almost as much time typing as I did talking to patients. Now I look at the person in front of me, not at a screen, and on a busy night the tool saves me close to an hour. Without it, I would not have gone back to sit with an elderly man who was frightened and alone last week. That hour matters.
>
> The drawbacks are just as real. The tool is not sentient, and it does not understand anything; it predicts words. Twice it wrote that a patient had denied chest pain when we had never discussed chest pain at all. Both times I caught the mistake, but only because I read every line. On a night when I was exhausted, I might not have. The danger is not that the machine makes errors; it is that it writes them so smoothly, as though it had heard every word. Accumulated over thousands of visits, even a small rate of such errors could undermine the integrity of our records.
>

## Bài đọc 3 (Unit 11) — Bài của cô Lydia Park: vì sao ta đổi cách nói theo người nghe

> **Why We Change the Way We Speak**
>
> Lydia Park, English Department, for the Westview High parents' newsletter
>
> Every September, one of my students asks me the same question in a slightly accusing tone: "Ms. Park, why do you talk differently on the phone with your mother?" The honest answer is that I do, and so, almost certainly, do you.
>
> Researchers in linguistics have studied this habit for decades. In the 1970s, the researcher Howard Giles proposed what is now known as communication accommodation theory: the idea that we constantly, and mostly without noticing, adjust our speech to the person in front of us. We slow down or speed up, borrow words, make our accents lighter or stronger, and change our level of formality, depending on whom we are talking to and what we want from the conversation. Much as we like to think that our voice belongs to us alone, it is in fact shaped by every conversation we have.
>
> Most of the time, these adjustments bring people together. Moving our speech towards someone else's, which is known in linguistics as convergence, tends to build rapport, and studies have found that speakers who do it are often judged as warmer and more pleasant. A nurse who drops the medical terms with a frightened patient, or a teenager who subtly picks up a new friend's expressions, is doing exactly this.
>
> Convergence can go wrong, however. Speak too slowly and simply to an elderly person who understands perfectly well, and the effort meant as kindness comes across as talking down to them. Imitate a regional dialect that you do not share, and it may sound less like friendship than an insult. The difference lies not in the adjustment itself but in whether it responds to the real person or to an assumption about a group they belong to.
>
> Sometimes we move the other way. Speakers may deliberately emphasize their own accent or vocabulary, a strategy called divergence, either to show that they belong to a particular community or to keep a polite distance. A colleague who keeps the dialect of the town where she grew up, wherever she happens to live, is by no means being rude; she is telling you something about who she is.
>
> Why does this matter for our students? Because a growing part of their lives will be spent talking to people whose backgrounds differ from their own, in class, at work and online. Linguistic skill at this level is not so much about grammar as about tact: noticing contextual clues, adjusting without exaggerating, and never assuming that someone needs easier language before they have shown that they do.
>
> So when your teenager speaks one way to you and another way to their friends, try not to worry. Whatever else it means, it shows that they are listening.

## Bài đọc 4 (Unit 11) — Bài kiến thức nhân học: những luật ngầm của việc tặng quà

> **The Hidden Rules of Giving**
>
> By Paul Lockwood, for the Ideas page
>
> A neighbor brings you a jar of jam she has made herself. You thank her warmly, and then, if you are like most people, you begin to feel a small but definite pressure: sooner or later, you will have to give her something back. Where does that pressure come from, and why is it so hard to ignore?
>
> Nearly a century ago, the anthropologist Marcel Mauss tried to answer that question in a short book called The Gift. Comparing records of exchange from many societies, some from antiquity and some from his own time, he concluded that gifts are almost never as free as they appear. Wherever he looked, he found the same three duties: to give, to receive and to give back. Refusing a gift could be as insulting as failing to offer one, because both meant rejecting the relationship the gift was meant to create.
>
> Mauss's idea has since been tested in surprisingly ordinary settings. In one well-known experiment in the 1970s, a researcher mailed holiday cards to hundreds of strangers whose names he had chosen at random. A startling number of them wrote back, some with long letters about their families, even though none of them had any idea who he was. So strong was the habit of giving back that it outweighed the obvious question of who this man might be.
>
> Businesses, not surprisingly, have noticed. Researchers who studied restaurant tips found that servers who left a mint with the bill received slightly bigger tips, and those who walked away and then turned back to offer a second mint, as though they had just decided that these customers deserved it, received clearly bigger ones. The free sample in a supermarket and the complimentary breakfast at a hotel work on the same principle: a small gift creates a feeling that something is owed.
>
> None of this means that kindness is simply a trade in disguise. Psychologists and anthropologists still debate whether truly altruistic behavior exists, and much depends on how it is defined. What Mauss's work does show is that gifts are, above all, a way of building and keeping relationships. That is why a gift that is far too expensive can make the person who receives it uncomfortable rather than flattered: it creates a debt that cannot easily be paid back.
>
> Seen in this light, the jar of jam is not a bill. It is an invitation to keep the relationship going, and the best response may simply be to accept it gladly and, when the moment feels right, to bake something in return.

## Bài đọc 5 (Unit 12) — Bài phân tích: khi máy học cả thói xấu của con người

> **When a Machine Learns Our Bad Habits**
>
> A briefing for readers of the Technology and Society page
>
> In 2018, a news report revealed that one of the world's biggest online retailers had quietly abandoned an experimental computer system designed to rank job applicants. The system had been trained on ten years of applications sent to the company, most of them from men, and it had learned, in effect, to treat being male as an indicator of a good engineer. Applications that mentioned the word "women's," as in "captain of the women's chess club," were consistently scored lower. Engineers tried to correct the problem, but they could not be sure that the system would not find other, less obvious ways to favor men, and the project was dropped.
>
> Nobody had programmed the machine to be unfair. That is precisely what makes cases like this so difficult. A system that learns from past decisions inherits whatever patterns those decisions contain, and if the past was unfair, the system will tend to perpetuate that inequality, now with the authority of a calculation.
>
> Who, then, is responsible when an algorithm treats people unfairly? The company that builds the tool may argue that it simply supplied software, while the employer that uses it may argue that it trusted an expert product. Meanwhile, the applicant who was rejected usually has no idea that a machine was involved at all, and by the time she thinks to ask, the decision has long been made. Left unchallenged, this gap allows everyone to point at someone else.
>
> Researchers and lawyers have proposed several answers, which differ more in emphasis than in substance. One is being open: employers should tell applicants when computer tools are used and on what basis. Another is testing: before and after a system is put to work, it should be checked regularly for results that differ between groups, much as a new bridge is inspected for structural weaknesses. A third is keeping a human in charge, so that a person with real authority reviews the machine's recommendations and can step in when something looks wrong.
>
> Each answer has its drawbacks. Being open means little if the explanation is too technical to understand, and testing is only as good as the data used for it. And a human reviewer who is given two hundred applications an hour is unlikely to do more than click "approve," as though the machine had already made the decision for them.
>
> Perhaps the most robust principle is also the easiest to state: an organization that chooses to use a tool should not be able to relinquish responsibility for what the tool does. A company would not escape liability by claiming that a manager had been following a bad set of instructions, and there is little reason it should escape by claiming that it was following a bad algorithm.

## Bài đọc 6 (Unit 12) — Thư góp ý của thầy Mitchell Carter về phần mềm theo dõi máy tính học sinh

> **Letter to the Westview High Technology Committee**
>
> From: Mitchell Carter, History Department. About: the plan to install FocusTrack on student laptops
>
> Dear members of the committee,
>
> Thank you for inviting staff to comment before you make your decision in November. I write as someone who has taught at Westview for many years and who shares the committee's concern about distraction in class; I simply do not believe that FocusTrack is the right answer to it.
>
> As I understand the plan, the software would record what appears on each student's screen during lessons, mark any activity it judges to be off task, and give every student a weekly "attention score" that teachers and parents could see. The company's materials present this as a neutral tool, but I would argue that it raises an ethical dilemma we should not pass over quickly.
>
> My first concern is accuracy. A student who opens a news website to check a date during a history lesson is on task, while a student who stares silently at a perfectly suitable page may not be. The software cannot differentiate between the two with any confidence, and yet its score would look precise and official. Numbers of this kind have a way of dictating decisions long after everyone has forgotten how rough they were.
>
> My second concern is what constant observation does to learning itself. History, at least as I try to teach it, depends on students being willing to ask awkward questions, follow an idea that turns out to be irrelevant, and sometimes be wrong in front of others. Being watched every minute, I suspect, would inhibit exactly those habits, and students would learn to look busy rather than to think.
>
> My third concern is privacy. Students' screens often show far more than schoolwork: messages from family, health information, a search they would rather keep to themselves. Collecting such sensitive material simply because it is technically possible is, in my view, problematic, regardless of how carefully the company promises to store it.
>
> I do not wish to criticize without offering an alternative. During our phone-free week last April, several colleagues and I found that clear expectations and well-planned lessons did more for attention than any rule could compel. I would therefore propose a one-semester trial in which teachers who wish to may use simple screen-sharing tools during specific tasks, with students' knowledge, and with no scores recorded or shared.
>
> To take a stance against this software is not to take a stance against technology. It is to insist that the tools we choose should support the trust between teachers and students rather than replace it. I would be glad to discuss any of this with the committee in person.
>
> Yours sincerely, Mitchell Carter

## 💡 Mẹo làm bài

- Ví dụ "ngược đời" (người thẳng tính nhất lại ở nơi ta không ngờ) là chỗ hỏi **chức năng ví dụ**: nó dùng để bác bỏ một cách nghĩ.
- `That does not mean X. It means Y.` → người viết **giới hạn** ý của mình. Phương án tuyệt đối (no influence at all / explains everything) thường sai.
- Bài quan điểm C1 hay có lập trường **có điều kiện**: `I would keep…, but only with…`. Câu hỏi về quan điểm phải chứa cả phần điều kiện.
- Điều kiện ẩn (`Without it, I would not have…`) = câu điều kiện loại 3. Câu hỏi hàm ý hỏi điều đã thực sự xảy ra nhờ có nó.
- Bài kiến thức ngôn ngữ học: `Much as we like to think…` thừa nhận một niềm tin phổ biến rồi **lật lại** ở vế sau — đáp án nằm ở vế sau.
- Câu mệnh lệnh + `and` (`Speak too slowly…, and the effort… comes across as…`) là một kiểu **điều kiện ngầm**: "nếu làm X thì sẽ Y".
- Bài phân tích có cấu trúc **vấn đề → ai chịu trách nhiệm → các lời giải → nhược điểm → kết luận**; câu hỏi về quan điểm tác giả thường nằm ở đoạn cuối.
- Thư góp ý chính thức: người viết hay **trả lời trước phản bác** (`To take a stance against X is not to…`) — nhận ra chức năng này để làm câu hỏi mục đích.
