## Bài đọc 1 (Unit 5) — Trevor viết về điện năng của AI

> **What Does It Cost to Ask a Machine?**
>
> Trevor Carter, first-year environmental engineering student, for the campus magazine Green Lines
>
> Last week, a friend in my dorm refused to use an AI chatbot for his homework, not because of cheating, but because of the planet. "Every question burns electricity," he said. Half of the people in the room laughed, and the other half looked guilty. As an engineering student, I wanted numbers, not feelings, so I looked for them.
>
> The most reliable source I found was a 2025 report by the International Energy Agency. It estimated that data centers, the huge buildings full of computers that run the internet and AI, used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024, roughly 1.5 percent of all the electricity used in the world. The same report expected that figure to more than double by 2030, to around 945 terawatt-hours, slightly more than the whole of Japan uses today, with AI as the main reason for the growth.
>
> So was my friend right? Yes and no. The electricity used by one question is negligible; it is far less than the energy needed to boil water for a cup of tea. Refusing to ask a chatbot about your homework will not alleviate climate change in any meaningful way. The problem is not any single question but scale: billions of questions a day, plus the enormous amount of computing needed to train each new model in the first place.
>
> Where that electricity comes from matters enormously. A data center powered by wind turbines and solar panels is a very different thing from one powered by fossil fuels, and many of the centers that will have been built by 2030 are still being planned today. Some technology companies have already signed long-term deals to buy electricity directly from power plants, which tells you how much they expect to need. The decisions being made now will therefore shape emissions for decades. It is also worth remembering that technology has enabled engineers to cut waste elsewhere; AI is already being used to manage power grids, whereby supply and demand are balanced more precisely.
>
> Here is my conclusion. Feeling guilty about each question is pointless, and it is detrimental in its own way, because it makes a huge problem look like a matter of personal shame. What is viable, and far more useful, is to ask the companies that build these systems to say clearly how much energy they use and where it comes from. Transparency is the first step towards a sustainable answer.
>
> My friend is still not convinced. He now does his homework in the library, under forty bright lights that nobody ever turns off.

## Bài đọc 2 (Unit 6) — Nhật ký Mitchell nhận chẩn đoán huyết áp cao

> **Fifty, and My Numbers Are Wrong**
>
> From the notebook of Mitchell Carter
>
> March 4. My annual checkup. I went in expecting to be told what I am always told: lose a few pounds, sleep more. Instead, Dr. Ramos measured my blood pressure three times, frowned, and said the word "hypertension". I told him that I feel perfectly fine. He said that is exactly the problem: high blood pressure is often not symptomatic at all, which is why so many people find out only after something serious has happened.
>
> March 5. Megan was quiet at dinner, and later she admitted that she was angry with herself. "I check other people's blood pressure twelve hours a day," she said. "I ought to have noticed yours." I told her that nobody measures their own husband for fun. She did not laugh.
>
> March 12. The plan has three parts: a small daily pill, a thirty-minute walk every day, and much less salt. The third part is the hardest. It seems strange that a man who cooks as carefully as I do should have been eating so much salt without knowing it. But when I read the labels in our kitchen, the answer was there: the bread, the soup cubes, the sauces I add "just to finish a dish". I must have been adding salt to almost everything for thirty years.
>

## Bài đọc 3 (Unit 5) — Bài khoa học: lớp đất sống dưới chân ta

> **The Living Ground Beneath Our Feet**
>
> A handful of soil does not look like much: it is brown, a little dirty, and very easy to ignore. Yet a single teaspoon of healthy soil can contain billions of microorganisms, along with tiny insects and worms that break down dead vegetation and turn it into food for plants. Almost everything we eat depends, directly or indirectly, on this thin layer of life, which in many places is less than a foot deep.
>
> Over the past century, modern farming has enabled the world to feed billions more people than anyone once thought possible. Chemical fertilizer, pesticides and large irrigation systems have made the cultivation of crops far more productive, and they are a major reason why famine has become much less common. However, the same methods, applied year after year on the same land, have had a hidden cost. When fields are plowed again and again and left bare between harvests, wind and rain carry the top layer away, and soil that took centuries to form can be lost in a few decades. According to a major report by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, about a third of the world's soils are already moderately or highly degraded.
>
> Degradation, however, is not only a matter of how much soil is lost. Heavy use of pesticides can wipe out the insects and worms that keep soil healthy, along with the natural predators that would otherwise keep pests under control. Fertilizer that the crops do not absorb is washed into rivers, where it can cause contamination far from the farm where it was first applied. Inevitably, farmers who see their harvests fall respond by applying even more chemicals, and the cycle continues.
>
> The encouraging news is that soil can recover, and often faster than scientists once expected. Some farmers now plant cover crops after the main harvest, so that living roots hold the soil in place through the winter instead of leaving it bare. Others have stopped plowing altogether, using a method whereby seeds are placed directly into the remains of the previous crop. Adding compost to the ground, or letting livestock feed on a field for short periods before moving them on, can also return valuable organic matter to the soil. None of these methods is new; in fact, many of them are older than the chemicals that replaced them.
>
> Recovery takes patience, however, and the first few years can be difficult for a farmer. Harvests may drop slightly while the soil rebuilds itself, which is why many experts argue that farmers should receive support during the change, rather than being asked to carry the whole cost alone. Some researchers warn that, if current trends continue, much of the world's best farmland will have been seriously damaged by the end of this century. If it is protected, on the other hand, the same land could go on feeding us indefinitely.
>
> The next time you walk across a field or a park, it may be worth remembering that the ground beneath your feet is not simply dirt. It is alive, it is working, and it is far easier to destroy than to rebuild.

## Bài đọc 4 (Unit 5) — Thư bà Harriet đề nghị nhà cung cấp cà phê đổi bao bì

> **A Proposal About Our Deliveries**
>
> Dear Mr. Daniels,
>
> I am writing on behalf of Harriet's, the small café on Maple Street that has bought its coffee from Coastline Coffee Company for more than twelve years. You may remember that you were very kind to us a few winters ago, when a broken delivery truck spoiled two of our orders, so I hope you will read this letter as coming from a loyal customer rather than a difficult one.
>
> My concern is packaging. Every week we receive our beans in twenty one-pound bags, each made of layers of plastic and metal, and every week we discard all twenty of them, because they cannot be recycled where we live. That comes to more than a thousand bags a year from one small café, which seems a great deal of waste for a product that is usually finished within a few days of arriving. My customers have started to notice, too; several of the regulars now bring their own cups, and one of them, a retired chemistry teacher, recently asked me, quite politely, why the café still throws away so much plastic.
>
> I would like to propose a simple swap. Instead of small bags, could Coastline deliver our beans in bulk, in durable containers that your driver would collect, empty, on the following delivery? I understand that a few other coffee companies already offer such a system, whereby the same containers are cleaned and used again for years. We would happily pay a small deposit on each container, and we are willing to order more at a time, which should make the arrangement cost-effective for both of us. If reusable containers are not a viable option for you at present, biodegradable bags that we could put in our compost would still be a considerable improvement.
>
> I realize that this may sound like a small matter to a company of your size. However, coffee is a staple of our business, just as bread and milk are, and the bag it comes in is the single biggest source of waste behind our counter. Only last month we stopped selling bottled water for the same reason, and, to my surprise, we did not lose a single customer. I am not asking you to change your whole business overnight; I am simply asking whether you would be willing to try this with one café, and I would be delighted if it were mine.
>
> Let me be clear that I have no wish to change suppliers, because your beans are the reason many of my customers come back, and I would much rather solve this problem together. I would be grateful for a reply by the end of April, and you are, of course, always welcome to visit the café and discuss it over a cup of your own coffee.
>
> Yours sincerely,
>
> Harriet Brooks, owner, Harriet's, Maple Street, San Diego
>
> P.S. My son, who is an engineer, tells me that a business letter should always include a figure, so here is one: our café alone has thrown away roughly thirteen thousand of your bags since we became your customer.

## Bài đọc 5 (Unit 6) — Bài khoa học: khi thuốc kháng sinh mất tác dụng

> **When the Pills Stop Working**
>
> In 1928, the Scottish scientist Alexander Fleming returned from a holiday to find that mold had grown on one of his laboratory dishes, and that the bacteria around it had died. The substance produced by that mold, penicillin, became the first widely used antibiotic, and it changed medicine so fundamentally that it is hard to imagine the world before it. Infections that had once been a common cause of death could suddenly be cured in a matter of days.
>
> Fleming himself saw the danger early. In his Nobel Prize lecture in 1945, he warned that people who took too little of the drug, or stopped too soon, might expose bacteria to it without killing them, and so teach them to survive. He was right. Whenever bacteria are exposed to an antibiotic, the most susceptible ones die first, while the few that happen to carry a genetic change that protects them survive and multiply. Repeat that process millions of times, in hospitals, homes and farms around the world, and you produce bacteria that are resistant to the very drugs designed to kill them.
>
> The scale of the problem is now hard to ignore. A large study published in The Lancet in 2024 estimated that infections resistant to antibiotics directly caused more than a million deaths in 2021, and that the figure could reach nearly two million a year by 2050 if nothing changes. Those numbers do not include the less visible costs. Many routine surgical procedures, from knee operations to organ transplants, are safe only because antibiotics can prevent infection afterwards, and the same is true of cancer treatments that weaken the immune system. Without effective drugs, operations that we now consider ordinary would become far more dangerous.
>
> Why not simply invent new antibiotics? Scientists are trying, but very few entirely new types have reached patients since the 1980s. Developing a drug can take more than a decade of research and clinical trials, and a new antibiotic earns relatively little money, because doctors are told to keep it in reserve and prescribe it only when older drugs have failed. In other words, the more valuable a new antibiotic is to public health, the less it is likely to be used, and the less attractive it becomes to the companies that would have to pay for it.
>
> That leaves prevention, and here individuals do have a role to play. Antibiotics do nothing against viruses, so asking for them for a cold or the flu does not help you and only adds to the problem. For decades, patients were also told to finish every course, even after they felt better, but in 2017 a group of infection specialists argued in a leading medical journal that there was surprisingly little evidence for this rule. Their point was not that people should stop whenever they like, but that courses should be no longer than necessary; the best advice is still to follow your doctor's instructions exactly. Penicillin was a gift that arrived almost by accident, and it would be a tragedy to lose it through carelessness.

>
> I should admit at the start that I am not the ideal reader for a book about rest. In more than twenty years of night shifts, I have learned to sleep in a parked car, eat standing up, and treat a day off as a chance to catch up on laundry. So when a colleague left Paula Whitman's Off Duty in my locker with a note that said simply, 'Read this. Then actually do it,' I suspected that I was being told something.
>
> Whitman, a sleep researcher who has worked with hospital staff for years, starts from a claim that sounds obvious but is easy to forget: rest is not simply the absence of work, but a separate activity that has to be planned. She distinguishes between several kinds of rest, including physical rest, mental rest and what she calls social rest, meaning time spent with people who do not need anything from you. Her chapter on sleep is the strongest in the book; it explains clearly how shift work upsets the hormones that control sleep and appetite, and why a short afternoon snooze can be more restorative than an extra hour in bed.
>
> What I valued most, however, was her refusal to treat well-being as a matter of personal discipline alone. Whitman points out that a nurse who is told to do stretching exercises and unplug in the evening, but who is then scheduled for three night shifts in a row, has not been given a well-being program; she has been given homework. It is the best sentence in the book, and I have already read it aloud to two managers. Her argument that staff need a sense of control over their own schedules, and not only longer breaks, deserves to be taken seriously by anyone who writes the schedules in this building.
>
> The book is not without weaknesses. The middle chapters, on daydreaming and creativity, are pleasant but thin, and they rely heavily on stories from Whitman's own life rather than on research. Some of her suggestions also assume far more free time than most of us have; I do not know many nurses who can spend an hour in a sauna on a weekday. And although she notes that a sedentary lifestyle and constant stress damage health, she says little about how to rest when you are worried about someone you love, which is precisely when rest is hardest.
>
> I have tried two of her ideas since finishing the book. The first is a ten-minute walk outside after each night shift, before driving home, which has turned out to be surprisingly effective. The second is confiding in a colleague instead of pretending to be fine, which has turned out to be much harder. My husband, who has his own reasons to take rest seriously this spring, says that I am calmer, although he also says that I now talk about sleep at dinner. I would recommend Off Duty to every member of staff, and especially to our managers: it is not a perfect book, but it asks the right questions, and it asks them of the right people.

## 💡 Mẹo làm bài

- Bài có số liệu: phân biệt **số đã đo** (`used … in 2024`) với **dự báo** (`expected … by 2030`). Câu hỏi hay bẫy bằng cách đổi hai con số cho nhau.
- Người viết tự hỏi tự đáp `Yes and no` → câu trả lời có hai nửa. Phương án đúng phải chứa **cả hai** nửa.
- Câu cuối hài hước (đèn thư viện) thường hỏi về **giọng điệu/hiệu ứng**, không phải về lập luận chính.
- Trong nhật ký, câu rất ngắn sau một câu đùa (`She did not laugh.`) là chỗ hỏi hàm ý: cảm xúc thật của nhân vật.
- Bài khoa học dạng vấn đề → giải pháp: câu chuyển ý (`However`, `The encouraging news is that…`) đánh dấu chỗ bài đổi hướng. Câu hỏi về cấu trúc hay hỏi đúng những câu này.
- Thư đề xuất: chi tiết nhắc lại quá khứ tốt đẹp ở đầu thư thường nhằm **giữ quan hệ**, không phải để phàn nàn. Hạn trả lời không phải lời đe doạ nếu người viết đã nói rõ không muốn đổi.
- Bài điểm sách: câu hỏi "NOT a weakness" thường cài phương án là phần được KHEN ở đoạn trước (`the strongest in the book`).
