## Bài đọc 1 (Unit 3) — Chuyên mục của Nathan: kiểm chứng một bức ảnh lan truyền

> **Ten Minutes Before You Share**
>
> Nathan Brooks, "Tech Corner", Maple Street Neighbors' Newsletter, February
>
> Last month, a dramatic photo went into circulation on our neighborhood group chat. It supposedly showed the Pine Street Library under a meter of brown water after the January storm, with books floating out of the front door. Within an hour, it had been shared fourteen times, including by my own mother, who added three crying faces and the words "Is this true??"
>
> It was not true. The photo had been made by an image generator, and it took me about ten minutes to find that out. I want to explain how, because the method matters more than this particular photo.
>
> A few years ago, the standard advice was to look for mistakes in the picture itself: a hand with six fingers, letters on a sign that make no sense. That advice is now out of date. Image generators have improved so quickly that, arguably, nobody can reliably spot a fake just by staring at it.
>
> A better method comes from a well-known 2017 study by researchers at Stanford University. They asked three groups, namely professional historians, university students and professional fact-checkers, to judge how credible various websites were. The historians and students read each page carefully from top to bottom, and they were often fooled by official-looking logos. The fact-checkers did something different: they left the page almost at once and opened new tabs to find out what other sources said about it. This is called "lateral reading", and the fact-checkers reached better conclusions in a fraction of the time.
>
> So I read laterally. First, who posted the photo? The account had been created only two weeks earlier, and it had posted dozens of similar flood pictures from cities all over the world. That an account so new had produced so much drama told me a great deal. Second, what did credible local sources say? Not one local news site had reported a flood, and the library's own page had posted a photo that morning of its staff, dry and smiling, opening the doors at nine.
>
> I am not saying this to make anyone feel foolish. My mother shared the photo because she loves that library, and that is precisely why such fakes work: they are designed to make us feel before we think. To my mind, the answer is not to trust nothing, which is just another way of being fooled. It is to take ten minutes, open a new tab, and ask who else is saying this. If nobody credible is, the photo can wait.

### Câu hỏi

**1. What did the viral photo supposedly show?**
*(Bức ảnh lan truyền được cho là chụp cảnh gì?)*

- A. Nathan's house flooded after the January storm *(nhà Nathan bị ngập sau cơn bão tháng Một)*
- B. the Maple Street Library covered in snow after the January storm *(thư viện Maple Street phủ đầy tuyết sau cơn bão tháng Một)*
- C. the Pine Street Library flooded after the January storm *(thư viện Pine Street ngập sau cơn bão tháng Một)*
- D. a library closing for good after it ran out of money *(một thư viện đóng cửa hẳn vì hết tiền)*

**2. Why does Nathan say he wants to explain how he checked the photo?**
*(Vì sao Nathan nói anh muốn giải thích cách anh kiểm tra bức ảnh?)*

- A. because the method is more important than this one photo *(vì phương pháp quan trọng hơn chính bức ảnh này)*
- B. because his mother asked him to show everyone how he found the photo *(vì mẹ anh nhờ anh chỉ cho mọi người cách anh tìm ra bức ảnh)*
- C. because he made the photo himself to show how easy it is *(vì chính anh làm ra bức ảnh để cho thấy việc đó dễ thế nào)*
- D. because the library paid him to write about the flood story *(vì thư viện trả tiền để anh viết về chuyện lũ)*

**3. According to Nathan, why is the old advice about six fingers and strange signs out of date?**
*(Theo Nathan, vì sao lời khuyên cũ về sáu ngón tay và biển hiệu lạ đã lỗi thời?)*

- A. because people no longer share photos on the neighborhood group chat *(vì người ta không còn chia sẻ ảnh trên nhóm chat của khu phố)*
- B. because fake photos have become so easy to recognize that nobody needs to check them any more *(vì ảnh giả đã trở nên dễ nhận ra đến mức không ai cần kiểm tra nữa)*
- C. because signs and hands are now hidden by the websites that carry the photos *(vì biển hiệu và bàn tay giờ bị các trang web đăng ảnh che đi)*
- D. because image generators have improved so much that staring at a picture is unreliable *(vì công cụ tạo ảnh tiến bộ tới mức nhìn chằm chằm vào ảnh không còn đáng tin)*

**4. How did the fact-checkers in the Stanford study judge websites?**
*(Những người kiểm chứng tin trong nghiên cứu Stanford đánh giá trang web thế nào?)*

- A. They read every page carefully from top to bottom before deciding. *(Họ đọc kỹ mỗi trang từ trên xuống dưới rồi mới quyết định.)*
- B. They quickly left the page to see what other sources said about it. *(Họ nhanh chóng rời trang để xem nguồn khác nói gì về nó.)*
- C. They looked carefully at the logos to decide which sites were official. *(Họ nhìn kỹ các logo để quyết định trang nào là chính thức.)*
- D. They asked a group of historians to help them judge each website. *(Họ nhờ một nhóm nhà sử học giúp đánh giá từng trang web.)*

**5. What can be inferred about the historians and students in the study?**
*(Có thể suy ra gì về các nhà sử học và sinh viên trong nghiên cứu?)*

- A. Their careful reading of a single page did not protect them from being fooled. *(Việc đọc kỹ một trang không giúp họ khỏi bị đánh lừa.)*
- B. They were faster than the fact-checkers at reaching a conclusion. *(Họ đưa ra kết luận nhanh hơn người kiểm chứng tin.)*
- C. They refused to take part once they saw the websites. *(Họ từ chối tham gia khi thấy các trang web.)*
- D. They knew almost nothing about history or about how websites work. *(Họ hầu như không biết gì về lịch sử hay cách các trang web hoạt động.)*

**6. What does the sentence beginning "That an account so new" suggest Nathan concluded?**
*(Câu bắt đầu bằng "That an account so new" cho thấy Nathan đã kết luận gì?)*

- A. The account belonged to a well-known local news site. *(Tài khoản thuộc về một trang tin địa phương có tiếng.)*
- B. The account was very popular because it posted so many photos. *(Tài khoản rất nổi tiếng vì đăng rất nhiều ảnh.)*
- C. The account was probably not a trustworthy source. *(Tài khoản có lẽ không phải nguồn đáng tin.)*
- D. The account had good photos taken by a professional. *(Tài khoản có những bức ảnh đẹp do người chuyên nghiệp chụp.)*

**7. Which piece of evidence most clearly showed that the photo was false?**
*(Bằng chứng nào cho thấy rõ nhất bức ảnh là giả?)*

- A. The photo had been shared fourteen times in less than an hour. *(Bức ảnh đã được chia sẻ mười bốn lần trong chưa đầy một giờ.)*
- B. Harriet added crying faces and asked whether the photo was true. *(Bà Harriet thêm biểu tượng mặt khóc và hỏi bức ảnh có thật không.)*
- C. No local news site reported a flood, and the library's page showed it open and dry. *(Không trang tin địa phương nào đưa tin lũ, và trang của thư viện cho thấy nó mở cửa, khô ráo.)*
- D. The storm happened in January, and the photo showed a meter of water. *(Cơn bão xảy ra vào tháng Một và bức ảnh cho thấy nước sâu một mét.)*

**8. Why does Nathan say he is "not saying this to make anyone feel foolish"?**
*(Vì sao Nathan nói anh "không nói ra điều này để khiến ai thấy mình ngớ ngẩn"?)*

- A. because he also shared the photo at first and now feels embarrassed about doing so *(vì lúc đầu anh cũng chia sẻ bức ảnh và giờ thấy xấu hổ về việc đó)*
- B. because he wants to be kind and explain why fakes work on people who care *(vì anh muốn tử tế và giải thích vì sao ảnh giả đánh trúng những người biết quan tâm)*
- C. because he is afraid his mother will be angry with him for writing about it *(vì anh sợ mẹ sẽ giận anh vì viết về chuyện này)*
- D. because the library asked him to be polite to people who shared the photo *(vì thư viện nhờ anh lịch sự với những người đã chia sẻ bức ảnh)*

**9. What is Nathan's position on trusting information online?**
*(Quan điểm của Nathan về việc tin thông tin trên mạng là gì?)*

- A. We should take a few minutes to check who else is reporting something before sharing it. *(Ta nên bỏ vài phút xem còn ai khác đưa tin trước khi chia sẻ.)*
- B. We should trust nothing at all, because anything online could be a fake. *(Ta không nên tin gì cả, vì mọi thứ trên mạng đều có thể là giả.)*
- C. We should trust photos more than words, since photos are harder to fake. *(Ta nên tin ảnh hơn chữ, vì ảnh khó làm giả hơn.)*
- D. We should stop using group chats, since fakes spread there so quickly. *(Ta nên bỏ nhóm chat, vì ảnh giả lan ở đó rất nhanh.)*

**10. How is the article organized?**
*(Bài viết được tổ chức thế nào?)*

- A. a list of rules with no examples, followed by a short story *(một danh sách quy tắc không có ví dụ, rồi đến một câu chuyện ngắn)*
- B. a story about the library's history, followed by advice for its readers *(một câu chuyện về lịch sử thư viện, rồi lời khuyên cho độc giả)*

## Bài đọc 3 (Unit 3) — Bài khoa học phổ thông: vì sao tin sai lan nhanh hơn tin thật

> **Faster, Further, Deeper: What a Decade of Rumors Reveals**
>
> Science Explained, a weekly column on research that matters.
>
> There is an old saying, often attributed to the writer Mark Twain, that a lie can travel around the world while the truth is still putting on its shoes. The irony is that there is no credible evidence that he ever said it, which is itself a small demonstration of how easily a good story spreads without anyone checking its source. For a long time, the saying was just that: a saying. Then, in 2018, three researchers at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology published what is still one of the biggest studies of misinformation ever carried out.
>
> The team sifted through about 126,000 stories that had been shared on a major social media platform between 2006 and 2017, each of which had been checked by independent fact-checking organizations and labeled as true or false. Together, these stories had been shared more than four and a half million times by around three million people. The findings were striking: false stories were about seventy percent more likely to be shared than true ones, and true stories took roughly six times as long to reach an audience of 1,500 people. On every measure the researchers used, false stories travelled faster, further and deeper into the network than the truth.
>
> The obvious suspect was automation. Surely, many people assumed, the false stories were being amplified by bots, the automated accounts that can share content thousands of times a day. Yet when the researchers removed the bots from their data, the pattern remained, because the bots, it turned out, shared true and false stories at roughly the same rate. That humans, not machines, were mainly responsible for the difference was arguably the study's most uncomfortable conclusion.
>
> Why would ordinary people prefer to share stories that are not true? The researchers' most plausible explanation is novelty: false stories tended to be more surprising than true ones, and people who share something new gain a little status as the person who knows first. The replies they analyzed bear this out, since responses to false stories expressed more surprise and fear, whereas responses to true stories expressed more sadness and trust.
>
> It would be a mistake to read too much into a single study, however large. The data came from one platform, the stories had to be ones that fact-checkers had chosen to investigate, and the way platforms rank content has changed considerably since 2017. Even so, its central finding has largely held up in later work, and it shifts the question in a useful direction. If the problem lay purely in algorithms, the solution would lie purely with engineers; if it lies partly in us, then the ten seconds before we press share matter more than any firewall.

## Bài đọc 4 (Unit 3) — Thư gửi toà soạn: quảng cáo đội lốt tin tức

> **Letter to the Editor: An Advertisement Dressed as News**
>
> To the Editor: I have read your paper every week for more than thirty years, and it is precisely because I value its credibility that I am writing to complain about an article in last Sunday's health section. The article, titled "The Morning Drink That Doctors Are Talking About", claimed that a powdered supplement called VitaCalm could reduce anxiety, improve sleep and support the brain in adults of every age. It quoted a leading nutrition expert, included a photo of a smiling customer, and ended with a discount code for readers. Only in very small grey letters at the bottom of the page did it say that this was sponsored content.
>
> I spent thirty-four years as a pharmacist, so I read the article with some care, and to my dismay I found that it did not stand up to even basic scrutiny. The leading expert turned out to be a paid adviser to the company that makes the product, a fact that the article glossed over entirely. The single study it cited had been paid for by the same company, had involved just forty people, and had allegedly shown a significant improvement on a questionnaire that the company itself had designed. None of this means that the product is harmful, but none of it is evidence that it works, either.
>
> I understand that newspapers need advertising, and I have no objection to advertisements as such. My objection is to the ploy of dressing an advertisement up as a news report, thereby borrowing the trust that your journalists have spent decades earning. A reader who looks quickly through the page, as most of us do, would have no reason to suspect that the article had been written to sell something. The irony is hard to miss: the very credibility that makes such content valuable to advertisers is what it slowly destroys.
>
> I would ask you to consider three changes. First, sponsored pages should be labeled at the top, in letters as large as the headline, not hidden at the bottom. Second, any expert quoted in such content should be clearly identified as someone paid by the advertiser. Third, health claims of this kind should be checked by your own science reporter before they are printed, just as any other claim in your pages would be. To my mind, these are modest requests, and they would cost far less than the readers you may otherwise lose. I look forward to reading next Sunday's paper, and I hope that I will be able to trust every page of it.
>
> Carol Jennings, San Diego

>
> Ask families why they moved out to the suburbs and the answers will be familiar: a spacious house, a garden, quieter streets and, quite often, a lower price. Ask the same families what they like least about their new lives, and many will name a single thing: the daily journey to work. Planners have a name for this tension, and economists have tried to put a price on it.
>
> In a widely cited 2008 study, the Swiss economists Alois Stutzer and Bruno Frey used survey data from thousands of households in Germany to compare how satisfied people were with their lives with how far they travelled to work. Standard economic theory predicts that long commutes should be balanced by other rewards, such as a higher salary or a bigger home, so that commuters end up no worse off than anyone else. The data did not bear this out. People with long commutes reported consistently lower satisfaction, and the researchers calculated that someone travelling an hour each way would need to earn roughly forty percent more to be as satisfied as a person who did not commute at all. They called this gap the commuting paradox.
>
> Why do so many people make a choice that apparently leaves them less satisfied? One plausible answer is that we judge homes and journeys in very different ways. A spacious living room is something that we notice every day, whereas we tend to imagine a commute on a good morning, with empty roads and a favorite podcast, rather than on a wet Monday with congestion clogging up every lane. Another is that the costs of commuting are spread thinly across thousands of small moments, each too minor to complain about, while the benefits of a bigger home arrive all at once, on the day we move in.
>
> The research has its limits. People who choose long commutes may differ in other ways from those who do not, and the data come from a single country, where public transit and the layout of cities differ from those in many American metropolitan areas. Later studies have also found that the type of journey matters: people who walk or cycle to work tend to report more satisfaction with their trip than drivers stuck in traffic.
>
> None of this means that moving to the suburbs is a mistake. For a family with three children, an extra bedroom may well be worth an hour a day in the car. What the commuting paradox suggests is simply that the hour should be counted honestly, at its real price, before the papers are signed, ideally with the journey tested on a weekday morning rather than a sunny Saturday afternoon.

## Bài đọc 6 (Unit 4) — Thư bà Harriet gửi chủ nhà về tiệm bánh bỏ trống

> **A Letter About the Empty Bakery**
>
> From: Harriet Brooks, owner, Harriet's Café, Maple Street. To: Mr. Raymond Dalton, Dalton Property Management. February 9.
>
> Dear Mr. Dalton, I am writing about the unoccupied shop adjoining my café, which has been empty since the bakery closed last July. I have been a good neighbor to that building for nearly fifteen years, and I would like to go on being one, but over the winter it has started to cause problems that I can no longer simply patch up myself. The most serious concerns the plumbing. Since the heavy rain in January, water has been coming through the wall that we share, and a dark stain now runs along the floor behind my counter. A carpenter who looked at it last week told me that the leak is almost certainly coming from an old pipe on your side, and that the longer it goes without repair, the more of the wall will need to be replaced. With the shop next door locked and nobody checking it, I have no way of knowing how much damage has already been done.
>
> The second problem is the air. The bakery's ventilation system has been switched off since it closed, and the smell of damp has begun to find its way into my café, which my customers have always described as warm and homely. Several of them have now told me, kindly but honestly, that the back room feels stuffy, and it is the very room where my book club and the Saturday homework group meet. I have opened the windows and bought a small machine to dry the air, but these are only temporary solutions to a problem that starts on the other side of the wall.
>
> Finally, a good deal of clutter has collected behind the building: broken chairs, empty boxes, and an old refrigerator that was probably left by the previous occupant. Children walk past that spot every afternoon on their way to the pocket park, and I would like it cleared before someone gets hurt.
>
> I would therefore ask you to arrange for a plumber to inspect the pipe this month, to have the ventilation switched on or checked, and to have the area behind the building cleared. I am, of course, happy to give your workers access through my back door.
>
> While I am writing, I should mention one more thing. When I telephoned your office last summer, I was told that the monthly rent would be 3,200 dollars, and I have been thinking about that figure more often than I expected. Once the repairs are done, I would very much like to see the space, perhaps with my son, who is better with numbers than I am. I am not promising anything, but at seventy-four I have learned that it is unwise to ignore an empty room next door for too long.
>
> Yours sincerely, Harriet Brooks

## 💡 Mẹo làm bài

- `supposedly`, `allegedly`, `reportedly` báo hiệu người viết **chưa tin** điều đi sau. Đây là gợi ý sớm cho câu hỏi thái độ.
- Câu hỏi cấu trúc bài: tìm các câu chuyển ý (`A better method comes from…`, `So I read laterally`, `To my mind`). Mỗi câu là đầu một bước lập luận.
- Email so sánh phương án: mỗi phương án có ưu → nhược (`The problem is…`). Câu hỏi nhược điểm lấy ở câu sau từ chuyển ý.
- `That being said` / `Having said that` = ý ngược lại sắp tới. Câu hỏi hàm ý thường nằm ngay sau cụm này.
- Bài khoa học phổ thông thường có đoạn `It would be a mistake to read too much into…` / `The research has its limits.` ngay trước kết luận. Câu hỏi mục đích đoạn cuối gần như luôn là: thừa nhận giới hạn nhưng giữ ý chính.
- Thư góp ý chính thức: người viết nêu tư cách (độc giả lâu năm, dược sĩ) để tạo độ tin cậy. Câu hỏi "Why does the writer mention…" thường hỏi đúng chức năng này.
- Câu hỏi NOT/EXCEPT: đối chiếu từng phương án với danh sách đề nghị (`First… Second… Third…` hoặc một câu liệt kê ba việc). Phương án sai thường là điều người viết nói rõ là KHÔNG muốn.
